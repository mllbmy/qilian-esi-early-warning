\title{Ecological security early warning in the Qilian Mountain region, China: temporal dynamics, forecasts, and spatial associations}

% Double-blind submission: the author block is intentionally omitted from the

% separate Title Page file.

\begin{abstract}
Mountain protected-area assessments often convert sparse environmental records into warning grades, but the effects of indicator construction and prediction choices remain uncertain. We assess ecological security in the Qilian Mountain region, China, using climate data and five land-cover epochs over 2005--2023. An eight-indicator DPSI index combines AHP and entropy weights, with Response retained as policy context. The reconstructed index peaks at 0.676 in 2010 and reaches 0.478 in 2023, classified as mild warning in 16 of 19 years. Removing the redundant ecological-land indicator leaves these grades unchanged, whereas removing the derived grassland change rate changes 11 grades and the peak year. GM(1,1), NGBM and ARIMA are compared with simple benchmarks in retrospective rolling forecasts of the reconstructed index. Their 2028 point projections are 0.448, 0.428 and 0.485. A mixture weighted by rolling five-year errors gives 0.459, with a central 95\% conditional bootstrap envelope of 0.249--0.779. This envelope describes the assumed residual process and model mix. Spatial analysis uses a separate six-indicator index at 1,914 grid points. Barren/degraded and grassland strata account for the largest shares of its spatial variation, but both overlap its construction. A separate NDVI product corroborates the spatial pattern ($r=0.705$, effective sample size approximately 23), while its regional annual variation differs from the reconstructed index. These results identify which warning features persist across indicator and model choices and support monitoring priorities conditional on spatial scale and index definition.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{Background}
Protected areas face linked pressures from climate and land-cover change \citep{wwf2020living,ipcc2014climate}. Mountain landscapes are especially difficult to monitor because climatic gradients, inaccessible terrain and multiple land uses occur over short distances. Ecological-security indices combine these dimensions into a summary that can be compared through time or across locations.

Field surveys, camera traps and remote sensing provide complementary observations for conservation \citep{kerry2022remote,bijl2022camera}. Translating these observations into a warning requires a definition of ecological condition, a reference scale and a rule connecting changes to management. Composite ecological-security assessments provide one such relative reference \citep{liu2023integrated,jinan_land_esp_2022}.

For sparse environmental records, the indicator definition is consequential. Interpolation creates annual trajectories between observation epochs, and a change-rate indicator may therefore describe the reconstruction as much as the underlying ecosystem. This study examines that dependence together with the sensitivity of forecasts and spatial associations in a national-park region.

\subsection{Research status and knowledge gaps}
The pressure--state--response (PSR) and DPSIR frameworks are widely used to organize ES indicator systems \citep{changbai_dpsirm_2024,dpsir_dea_threegorges2023}. ES assessments at mountain and reserve scale include the Changbai Mountain region, Yunnan's underdeveloped areas, and the adjacent Lanzhou--Xining urban agglomeration \citep{lu2014changbai,ou2017yunnan,tong2020lanzhou}. Subjective weighting (AHP) \citep{saaty1980analytic}, objective weighting (entropy method), and their combinations are mainstream. Grey models (GM(1,1)) \citep{deng1982control} are favored for small-sample ES time series; Liu et al.\ integrated DPSIR, combined weights, GM(1,1), and GeoDetector into a reserve-scale early-warning framework \citep{liu2023integrated}. The GeoDetector method \citep{wang2016measure,wang2012environmental} quantifies the strength of spatial association between individual factors and ES heterogeneity \citep{westliaohe_esp_2026,china_ecoenv_2024,qilian_ez_2024,zhang2019geodetector}.

Several choices can alter the interpretation of reserve-scale assessments. A single weighting scheme obscures sensitivity to the subjective--objective balance. Derived or compositionally redundant indicators can count the same environmental information more than once. Forecast comparison requires common time origins and simple benchmarks, and a factor that also enters the target index has a construction-related association with it. Examining these choices together can distinguish persistent patterns from those that depend on one index specification.

\subsection{Objectives of this study}
This study (1) reconstructs the regional ecological-security index and relative warning grades over 2005--2023, (2) evaluates their sensitivity to indicator selection, interpolation, weighting and thresholds, (3) compares retrospective forecasts of the reconstructed index and produces conditional projections for 2024--2028, and (4) examines spatial associations and independent NDVI consistency using a separately defined point-scale index. County-window comparisons provide context for monitoring priorities across the wider park region.

\section{Study area and data}
\label{sec:study}

\subsection{Study area}
The Qilian Mountain region, centered on the Qilian Mountain National Park (QMNP), is located in the northeastern margin of the Qinghai--Tibet Plateau, spanning the border of Gansu and Qinghai provinces (Fig.~\ref{fig:area}). The park protects typical alpine ecosystems, including glaciers, snowfields, alpine meadows, and desert steppes, which provide critical water services for the Hexi Corridor and inland river basins \citep{gao2023water}. We analyze a rectangular region ($36.0$--$40.5^{\circ}$N, $93.5$--$103.5^{\circ}$E) centered on the park and its buffer counties; conclusions are interpreted at the regional scale. The study region has an annual mean temperature of 0.8--2.0\,$^{\circ}$C and annual precipitation of 167--284\,mm over 2005--2023. Analysis units include the whole region and 11 counties (Tianzhu, Sunan, Subei, Aksai, Minle, Shandan, Menyuan, Qilian, Gangcha, Delingha, Tianjun).

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{DPSI weighted framework with an unweighted Response background}
\label{sec:dpsir}
Following the DPSIR-informed framing \citep{changbai_dpsirm_2024,dpsir_dea_threegorges2023}, nine candidate indicators cover the criteria layers: Driving forces (precipitation, temperature), Pressure (barren/degraded proportion, cropland proportion), State (grassland, forest, water proportions), Impact (grassland change rate), and Response (ecological land proportion). The set relies exclusively on open, reproducible data (CRU TS 4.10 climate; CLCD 30\,m land cover) and captures both the climate forcing and the land-surface response that dominate ecological security in arid--semiarid alpine ecosystems. Temperature is assigned a positive direction because heat limits vegetation growth in this cold setting; reversing the direction and dropping the indicator entirely are both reported as sensitivities (Sec.~\ref{sec:tempdir}). Table~\ref{tab:indicators} lists all indicators with their directions and states, row by row, which of them carry weight. Only the eight indicators marked as ESI inputs enter the weighted index; the Response-layer ecological land proportion is retained as an unweighted policy background and as the nine-indicator comparison specification, so the weighted index is a DPSI index that is informed by, rather than identical with, the DPSIR chain in which the Response layer is the policy-feedback element. Fig.~\ref{fig:framework} outlines how the DPSIR causal chain connects to the ESI-based warning pipeline; all indicators are min--max normalized to $[0,1]$ before weighting (Sec.~\ref{sec:esi}).

Three deterministic dependencies exist within this set and follow from the indicator definitions (Sec.~\ref{sec:deredundancy}). (i) The Response-layer ecological land proportion equals grassland $+$ forest $+$ water, with a residual of exactly zero: it is an exact linear combination of the three State columns. (ii) The Impact-layer grassland change rate is the annual rate of change of the State-layer grassland proportion, i.e. a derivative of a state variable, and its 2005 value is a convention of the differencing step rather than an observation. (iii) The Pressure-layer barren/degraded proportion equals $100-$ grassland $-$ forest $-$ water $-$ cropland, also with a zero residual, and correlates with the grassland proportion at $r=-0.92$; because the interannual ranges of forest, water and cropland are small, this column is close to a rescaled copy of the grassland proportion. The first identity repeats a linear combination of existing information, so the eight-indicator set obtained by deleting the ecological land proportion is adopted as the primary specification; the derivative and the complement are retained in the primary set for comparability with the DPSIR structure and are examined as sensitivities. At the point scale the barren/degraded indicator is the complement $1-$grassland$-$forest$-$water of a land-cover table without a cropland column, so 81 of the 1{,}914 samples that are dominated by cropland (75) or snow/ice (6) are counted as barren/degraded (Sec.~\ref{sec:spatial}).

\subsection{Combined weighting (AHP and entropy)}
Entropy weights are $e_j=-\frac{1}{\ln n}\sum_i p_{ij}\ln p_{ij}$ with $p_{ij}=x'_{ij}/\sum_i x'_{ij}$ and $w^{E}_j=(1-e_j)/\sum_j(1-e_j)$. AHP weights $w^{A}_j$ follow the principal-eigenvector method \citep{saaty1980analytic}. Because ecological security is most directly indicated by the State and Response layers, the criterion-level pairwise comparison matrix (five criteria on the Saaty 1--9 scale; Table~\ref{tab:ahp}) was derived from the DPSIR hierarchical priorities (State $>$ Response $>$ Impact $>$ Driving $=$ Pressure). This yields $\lambda_{\max}=5.015$, $CI=(\lambda_{\max}-n)/(n-1)=0.0038$ and $CR=CI/RI=0.0034<0.1$ ($RI=1.12$ at $n=5$), satisfying the consistency requirement. Within each criterion, indicators share the criterion weight equally. The combined weight is
\begin{equation}
w_j=\alpha\,w^{A}_j+(1-\alpha)\,w^{E}_j,\qquad \alpha\in[0,1],
\label{eq:weight}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha=0.4$ baseline and a full sensitivity analysis over $\alpha=0$--$1$ to test robustness to the subjective--objective balance. Entropy weights measure the interannual dispersion of an indicator rather than its ecological importance, which is consequential here because the grassland change rate is a difference of an interpolated series: its entropy weight ranges from 0.117 to 0.245 across interpolation schemes (linear, cubic spline, PCHIP, and the five observation epochs taken alone), and from 0.127 to 0.260 for the eight-indicator set, whereas no other indicator's entropy weight varies by more than 0.06 (Sec.~\ref{sec:weights}).

\subsection{Ecological security index (ESI)}
\label{sec:esi}
Indicators are first min--max normalized into $[0,1]$:
\begin{equation}
x'_{ij}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{x_{ij}-\min_j}{\max_j-\min_j}, & \text{positive},\\[2mm]
\dfrac{\max_j-x_{ij}}{\max_j-\min_j}, & \text{negative},\end{cases}
\label{eq:norm}
\end{equation}
and the ESI is the weighted sum
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{ESI}_i=\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_j\,x'_{ij},\qquad \mathrm{ESI}\in[0,1].
\label{eq:esi}
\end{equation}
Because the weights are time-invariant, the change between two years decomposes exactly into per-indicator contributions, $\Delta\mathrm{ESI}=\sum_j w_j\,\Delta x'_j$, with no weight-change term; this decomposition is used to interpret the 2005 minimum and the 2010 peak (Sec.~\ref{sec:tempdir}).

\subsection{Prediction models and warning classification}
\label{sec:warning}
The GM(1,1) model \citep{deng1982control} is built on the first-order accumulated series with least-squares estimates $(\hat a,\hat b)$:
\begin{equation}
\hat x^{(0)}(k+1)=\left(1-e^{\hat a}\right)\left(x^{(0)}(1)-\frac{\hat b}{\hat a}\right)e^{-\hat a k}.
\label{eq:gm}
\end{equation}
GM(1,1) represents an exponential response. The NGBM extends that response through $x^{(0)}(k)+az^{(1)}(k)=b[z^{(1)}(k)]^{\gamma}$, with $z^{(1)}$ the adjacent mean of the accumulated series. Its exponent is selected by minimum training residual sum of squares on a grid from 0.05 to 1.95 with step 0.05, excluding the singular value 1. The accumulated response uses time zero for the first observation. Restored fitted values end at time $n-1$, and the first forecast uses the next difference, at time $n$. ARIMA has fixed order $(0,1,1)$. The last-value (naive) and drift methods provide benchmarks \citep{hyndman2021forecasting}. Full-series fitting accuracy is summarized by RMSE, MAE, posterior-variance ratio $C=S_2/S_1$ and small-error probability $P$.

The forecasting task is retrospective prediction of a fixed, reconstructed index. The land-cover interpolation, normalization ranges and entropy weights define that target using the full 2005--2023 record. Each forecasting model is then fitted on years up to its origin, and the NGBM exponent is selected within that model-training window. Three error summaries are reported: a 2018-origin forecast of 2019--2023, rolling one-year forecasts with origins 2013--2022, and rolling five-year forecasts with origins 2013--2018. The five-year scheme contains six origins and 30 origin--horizon combinations, with overlapping target years. The inverse RMSE over those combinations determines the normalized weights of the three-model mixture. These weights describe performance on the reconstructed target and its five-year horizon. The annual land-cover values preceding some origins use later observation epochs, so this task represents retrospective index prediction rather than a deployment-time warning evaluation.

For the 2024--2028 projection, each model and the NGBM exponent are estimated from the complete 2005--2023 index. The projection continues its fitted statistical process. Residual resampling characterizes uncertainty conditional on that process. For each model, fitted and observed indices are transformed to the logit scale, the initial-condition residual is excluded, and the remaining residuals are centered. Circular moving blocks of length three generate pseudo-histories, which are inverse-transformed and refitted with the same estimator, including exponent selection. Each of 2,000 replicates also receives an independent resampled block sequence whose cumulative sum is added on the logit scale to represent accumulated future disturbances. The inverse logit bounds every simulated path in $(0,1)$. Parameter-only resampling is retained as a sensitivity. A linear pool samples the three model distributions in proportion to the mixture weights. The central 95\% of these simulations is reported as a conditional bootstrap envelope, and grade frequencies as bootstrap shares. Their scope is the specified residual construction, without an empirical coverage guarantee.

Five relative grades partition the normalized range: safe $[0.8,1]$, relatively safe $[0.6,0.8)$, mild warning $[0.4,0.6)$, medium warning $[0.2,0.4)$ and severe warning $[0,0.2)$. These equal-interval conventions follow reserve-scale index assessment \citep{liu2023integrated}. Grade interpretation is accompanied by the continuous index, threshold sensitivity and model spread.

\subsection{Spatially stratified association analysis (GeoDetector)}
The spatial target is a six-indicator cross-sectional index, distinct from the regional eight-indicator time series. At 1,914 regular 0.15$^{\circ}$ grid points it combines 2005--2023 mean precipitation and temperature with the 2020 dominant-class indicators for grassland, forest and water, and the complement $1-$grassland$-$forest$-$water. The land-cover class is the dominant CLCD category within a $\pm0.02^{\circ}$ neighborhood. These six inputs are min--max normalized across points. Their entropy weights are combined with equal subjective weights of $1/6$ at $\alpha=0.4$. This spatial index therefore has different inputs, normalization support and subjective weights from the regional time series. The complement counts 75 cropland and six snow/ice-dominated points as barren/degraded; the separately sampled CLCD class-7 factor retains its own definition.

GeoDetector measures the variance associated with a factor's strata \citep{wang2016measure,wang2012environmental}:
\begin{equation}
q=1-\frac{\sum_{h=1}^{L}N_h\sigma_h^2}{N\sigma^2},
\label{eq:q}
\end{equation}
where $N_h$ and $\sigma_h^2$ are the size and variance of stratum $h$, and $N$ and $\sigma^2$ are those of the full target. Climate factors use four quantile strata, whereas the binary dominant-class grassland and barren factors have two nonempty strata. Baseline label permutation uses 999 iterations and is reported as a nominal i.i.d. reference. Joint strata produce the interaction-detector statistic. Because joint stratification refines the single-factor partitions, a larger joint $q$ is interpreted as a descriptive joint association. Target-overlap diagnostics remove components or compare separate climate and land-cover sub-indices. They describe the dependence on index construction, without assigning a numerical fraction of association to ecological or mechanical sources.

Spatial alignment is additionally assessed against every nonidentity toroidal translation of the 29-by-66 target lattice. The 1,913 translations define a finite periodic-boundary reference. The tail fraction includes the observed alignment in its denominator. This reference changes alignment while retaining values, but its periodic boundary introduces artificial edge adjacencies, and it is treated as an alignment diagnostic under that assumption \citep{mrkvicka2021shift}. Pearson agreement with independent NOAA CDR growing-season NDVI is evaluated at points with valid retrievals. The Clifford--Richardson--Hemon correlation-variance approximation supplies an effective sample size and Fisher-transformed confidence interval, with lag support of at least 10\% of the sample and a main cutoff of ten grid cells \citep{clifford1989spatial}. Cutoffs of five to 20 cells describe sensitivity. This correlation correction is applied to Pearson $r$, while the $q$ analyses retain their descriptive and alignment-reference interpretation.

Sensitivity analyses vary interpolation, indicator sets, factor stratifications, point sampling, spatial weights and 64 county-window configurations.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Fig.~\ref{fig:trends} shows the raw indicator dynamics behind the ESI. Precipitation fluctuates between 166.9 and 284.4\,mm\,yr$^{-1}$ and falls from 228.0 (2005) to 166.9\,mm (2023); mean temperature rises from 1.29 to 1.92\,$^{\circ}$C. The land-cover indicators, linearly interpolated between the five CLCD epochs, change slowly: ecological land proportion (grassland, forest, and water combined) rises from 37.9\% in 2005 to 39.6\% in 2010 and remains near 39.0\% in 2023, the barren/degraded proportion declines from 58.2\% to 56.3\% and then returns to 57.0\%, water (1.75--2.17\%, peaking in 2020 and declining thereafter) and forest (1.15--1.29\%) proportions increase overall, and cropland grows slightly (3.8--4.0\%). Grassland change rate is the most volatile signal ($-0.47$ to $+0.84$\%\,yr$^{-1}$). After normalization and combined weighting (Sec.~\ref{sec:esi}), these series yield the ESI trajectory analyzed below (Fig.~\ref{fig:esi}).

\subsection{Indicator weights}
\label{sec:weights}
Entropy weighting (Table~\ref{tab:weights}) identifies the grassland change rate ($w^{E}=0.260$) and the cropland proportion ($w^{E}=0.181$) as the most informative signals of the eight-indicator primary set, and the AHP criterion weights emphasize the three State indicators (grassland, forest and water; 0.195 each) and the Impact-layer grassland change rate (0.198). Removing the exactly duplicated ecological land column redistributes its weight by two rules that are stated here because they are not symmetric. The entropy weights are \emph{not} recomputed: because entropy weighting depends only on the normalised matrix and on column totals, deleting one column changes every surviving weight through the common denominator $1-w^{E}_{\mathrm{X9}}$ alone, so all eight weights are multiplied by the same factor $1.062653$ (the eight ratios span $2\times10^{-16}$), and this closed form reproduces a numerical recomputation to $3\times10^{-8}$. The same device is \emph{not} available for the AHP weights, whose four surviving criteria sum to 0.749769 after the Response layer is removed, so retaining the original values would break $\sum_j w_j=1$. Rule~A therefore renormalises the surviving criterion weights (Driving, Pressure, State, Impact) back to one, which multiplies every surviving AHP weight by $1.333744$; re-solving the $4\times4$ surviving judgement matrix instead (rule~D) gives $\lambda_{\max}=4.004$ with CR $=0.0015$ and differs from rule~A by at most 0.0122, in the State layer. Under rule~A the four live layers carry 15.4\% (Driving), 19.0\% (Pressure), 42.1\% (State) and 23.5\% (Impact) of the combined weight and sum to 1.000000, and the overlap group that motivated the de-duplication (State plus Impact) carries 65.6\%, against 69.3\% for the same group while the duplicated Response column was still present. The combined weights ($\alpha=0.4$) rank the grassland change rate (0.235), the water proportion (0.151), the forest proportion (0.144) and the cropland proportion (0.131) highest, so grassland dynamics, water availability and forest extent account for the largest shares of the weighted temporal variation of the ESI (Fig.~\ref{fig:weights}). The high entropy weight of the grassland change rate is largely an artefact of how the land-cover series is interpolated: it falls from 0.260 under linear interpolation to 0.246 under a cubic spline, 0.184 under PCHIP and 0.127 when only the five observation epochs are used, whereas no other indicator changes by more than 0.06 across the same four schemes (\ref{app:interp}). Entropy weighting responds to interannual variability, and a differenced interpolated series is the most variable column in the system; the weight therefore does not measure the ecological importance of grassland dynamics, and it is reported alongside the scheme it came from.

\subsection{ESI time series and warning grades (2005--2023)}
\label{sec:esi-series}
Under the primary eight-indicator specification, the regional ESI rises from 0.289 in 2005 to 0.676 in 2010, then fluctuates between 0.423 and 0.530 over 2011--2023 and reaches 0.478 in 2023 (Fig.~\ref{fig:esi}). The nine-indicator comparison assigns the same grades in all 19 years: one medium-warning year, two relatively-safe years and 16 mild-warning years. Its largest absolute difference from the primary index is 0.058, with a mean absolute difference of 0.034. The agreement concerns these relative grades, while the continuous trajectories and their trend tests retain specification dependence.

Trend testing is reported under three variance conventions and three indicator specifications (Table~\ref{tab:trend}). Under the eight-indicator primary specification the conventions do not agree. The conventional Mann--Kendall test gives $Z=-1.33$ ($p=0.184$) for 2005--2023 and $Z=-1.31$ ($p=0.189$) for 2010--2023, and the Hamed--Rao autocorrelation-corrected test gives $p=0.193$ and $p=0.185$; under both, the Sen slope is small and its confidence interval spans zero ($-0.0031$\,yr$^{-1}$, 95\% CI $-0.0078$ to $+0.0017$; and $-0.0035$\,yr$^{-1}$, CI $-0.0117$ to $+0.0013$). Trend-free pre-whitening instead returns a significant full-period decline ($p=0.012$) and a clearly non-significant segment ($p=0.200$). The difference follows from how each correction treats serial dependence on a record of 19 years: Hamed--Rao keeps the Mann--Kendall statistic and inflates its variance by the estimated autocorrelation, whereas pre-whitening removes the lag-1 autocorrelation from the series before testing and then re-inserts the trend, which on this record leaves the residual monotone component with a smaller variance and hence a smaller $p$. Because the two dependence-correcting conventions disagree, the full-period decline is reported as significant under trend-free pre-whitening only, and as suggestive rather than established under the conventional Mann--Kendall and Hamed--Rao tests. The nine-indicator baseline is retained as a contrast and behaves differently: over the full record it is again non-significant under Mann--Kendall ($Z=-1.12$, $p=0.263$) and Hamed--Rao ($p=0.076$) but significant under pre-whitening ($p=0.010$), whereas its 2010--2023 segment is significant under all three conventions ($p=0.006$, 0.006 and 0.002; Sen $-0.0086$\,yr$^{-1}$, CI $-0.0153$ to $-0.0037$), with a Hamed--Rao effective sample size of only 0.40 of $n$, that correction being numerically unstable on records of this length. The post-2010 decline is therefore a property of the nine-indicator specification and does not survive removal of the duplicated ecological land column. A six-indicator specification that also drops the barren complement reverses the sign of the trend over the full record, so neither the sign nor the significance of the trend is specification-free. The tested series is also reconstructed rather than observed annually: five land-cover epochs are measured and the intervening 14 years are linearly interpolated (Sec.~\ref{sec:data}), and the leading temporal indicator is a change rate of that interpolated series, so these tests describe the trend of the reconstructed series and not of annually observed land-cover change.

Perturbing each of the four grade boundaries by $\pm$0.05 (eight scenarios) leaves the 2023 grade unchanged in every scenario. Under the primary specification only two boundaries matter: lowering the 0.6 boundary by 0.05 moves 2007 to relatively safe, raising it by 0.05 returns 2009 to mild warning, and raising the 0.4 boundary by 0.05 moves 2020 to medium warning; the 0.2 and 0.8 boundaries change no year. Under the nine-indicator specification the same perturbation changes 7 year-grades in total, with the largest single scenario changing 6 years (2007, 2011--2015). Both counts are small relative to a 19-year record but are not zero, which is why grades are reported together with the continuous ESI rather than as a stand-alone signal.

\subsection{Indicator independence and de-redundancy}
\label{sec:deredundancy}
Three algebraic dependencies follow from the indicator definitions (Sec.~\ref{sec:dpsir}). Ecological land equals grassland $+$ forest $+$ water. Grassland change rate equals $100(G_t/G_{t-1}-1)$, with its initial value set to zero. Regional barren/degraded is the residual $100-$grassland$-$forest$-$water$-$cropland. The archived rounded table reproduces the first two identities to within $5\times10^{-4}$. The residual is broader than the mapped CLCD barren class. The six classes listed in the regional source table sum to 41.97--43.85\%, leaving 56.15--58.03\% unlisted; the residual used in the index also absorbs listed shrub and impervious cover. At point scale the index defines barren/degraded as $1-$grassland$-$forest$-$water at all 1,914 samples. Of the 1,122 points with zero values for these three components, 81 are mapped to cropland (75) or snow/ice (6), showing that the residual is not a land-cover class label. At county scale the corresponding residual differs from the CLCD class-7 proportion by up to 2.01 percentage points. These differences motivate the separate indicator-set and land-cover-source sensitivity analyses.

Table~\ref{tab:deredundancy} reports the nine-indicator baseline and five alternative specifications. Deleting the redundant ecological land proportion leaves all 19 warning grades unchanged, keeps the peak year at 2010, and shifts the ESI by at most 0.058 (mean absolute difference 0.034); it is adopted as the primary specification, and it also widens the range of $\alpha$ over which no grade changes (Sec.~\ref{sec:alpha}). Deleting the grassland change rate instead moves the peak to 2017 and changes 11 of 19 grades, deleting it together with the ecological land proportion moves the peak to 2019 and changes 8 grades, and a six-indicator set that drops the barren complement as well changes 6 grades; the substituted five-indicator set keeps the peak year at 2010 but changes 11 grades and moves the 2023 ESI to 0.305 (medium warning). Because the six-indicator series is essentially uncorrelated with the nine-indicator series in rank (Spearman $\rho=-0.02$), the temporal narrative of a 2010 peak followed by a mild-warning plateau is not independent of the choice of indicator set; in particular it requires either the grassland change rate or the derived ecological land and barren terms to be retained, so it is a property of the DPSIR specification adopted here rather than a specification-free finding. Retaining the grassland change rate is a defensible choice for a conservative specification comparable with other DPSIR studies, but its entropy weight is largely an artefact of interpolating the land-cover series (Sec.~\ref{sec:weights}), which makes its numerical contribution conditional on the reconstruction scheme. The proportion of combined weight carried by the State, Impact and Response layers falls from 69.3\% to 65.6\% when the duplicated column is removed.

\subsection{Forecast comparison on the reconstructed index}
\label{sec:forecast}

The full-series fitting diagnostics in Table~\ref{tab:forecast} describe fit to the reconstructed record, whereas the rolling errors describe prediction of its later values from earlier segments.

The 2028 projections are 0.448, 0.428 and 0.485, and their mixture is 0.459 (Fig.~\ref{fig:esi}). All four point values fall in mild warning. The crossing of that threshold therefore depends on the exponent-selection window.

\subsection{Robustness to the weighting ratio $\alpha$}
\label{sec:alpha}
Sensitivity analysis over $\alpha=0$--$1$ shows that the 2023 ESI of the nine-indicator baseline increases from 0.425 (pure entropy, $\alpha=0$) to 0.578 (pure AHP, $\alpha=1$) (Fig.~\ref{fig:alpha}), while its warning grade remains mild warning throughout; under the eight-indicator primary specification the same endpoints are 0.415 and 0.573. The historical grade distribution is sensitive to $\alpha$: the number of mild-warning years falls from 16 at $\alpha=0.3$--$0.4$ to 5 at $\alpha\ge0.9$, and one severe-warning year appears from $\alpha\ge0.7$ (Table~\ref{tab:alpha}; the complete $11\times19$ matrix for both specifications is Table~\ref{tab:alpha-full} in \ref{app:alpha}). Under the eight-indicator primary specification, by contrast, the grade changes in no year for $\alpha$ between 0.4 and 0.8, in one year at $\alpha=0.9$ and in four years at $\alpha=1.0$, and the 2023 grade is mild warning at every $\alpha$ from 0 to 1. The duplicated ecological-land column is thus the main amplifier of the $\alpha$ sensitivity, because AHP gives that column a weight of 0.250 on its own. The baseline $\alpha=0.4$ lies in the widest stable interval under both specifications, so the current-state conclusion is robust to the subjective--objective balance; the historical grade distribution, however, is not, and is reported as $\alpha$-conditional.

\subsection{County-level relative index}
\label{sec:county}
The static county index for 2020 uses the same combined weighting ($\alpha = 0.4$) as the regional analysis, with entropy weights recomputed from the 11-county cross-section and AHP weights set equal across the six county-level indicators available; the inputs are the 2020 land-cover proportions and the 2005--2023 climate means for a $\pm$0.1$^{\circ}$ centroid window, min--max normalised across the 11 units. It ranks Sunan (0.558) and Tianzhu (0.525) highest and Aksai (0.067) lowest (Table~\ref{tab:county}). Three sources of uncertainty were quantified over 64 combinations of window half-width ($\pm$0.05$^{\circ}$ to $\pm$0.30$^{\circ}$), spatial weight (entropy, AHP, equal) and land-cover source (CLCD class 7, residual-barren formula, no-barren composition). Multi-year county indices cannot be computed from the data used here, because only the 2020 CLCD tile can be reprocessed alongside this repository and no county-boundary-consistent land-cover series was available for the other four epochs. County values are therefore used only as a coarse east--west contrast, and the management zoning of Sec.~\ref{sec:management} rests on the functional zoning of the national park together with the grade--response relationship rather than on county ranks.

\subsection{Spatially stratified associations}
\label{sec:spatial}
The six-indicator point index shows its largest associations with barren/degraded ($q=0.802$) and grassland ($q=0.708$), followed by precipitation (0.506) and temperature (0.170) (Fig.~\ref{fig:qbar}). All four nominal i.i.d. permutation values are 0.001. Target overlap contributes to these associations: removing the tested component gives $q=0.711$ for barren/degraded, 0.381 for precipitation, 0.366 for grassland and 0.274 for temperature. With both the grassland component and its complement removed from the target, the grassland association falls to $6.3\times10^{-5}$. These target changes demonstrate dependence on index construction. Cross-block comparisons provide additional diagnostic associations, without a decomposition of $q$ into ecological and mechanical fractions.

Elevation and slope, which are outside the index inputs, have $q=0.090$ and 0.065, and their joint stratification has $q=0.166$. An ordinary regression of the point index on elevation and slope gives $R^2=0.081$. Across six stratification schemes precipitation $q$ ranges from 0.506 to 0.541 and temperature from 0.159 to 0.196. Binary grassland and barren/degraded strata give the same $q$ across these schemes. Point resampling describes sample sensitivity (Table~\ref{tab:strat}), while the alignment analysis below uses the full spatial lattice.

The six joint-factor statistics exceed their larger single-factor statistic (Table~\ref{tab:interaction}; Fig.~\ref{fig:interaction}). None exceeds the sum of its two single-factor statistics. Joint stratification refines the constituent partitions, so this pattern describes joint variance association with the constructed target. Forest is treated separately: 15 samples are forest-dominated, binary stratification gives $q=0.065$, and zero-inflated fractional-cover stratification gives 0.206. Its concentration and the target's entropy weighting make its contribution sensitive to spatial representation.

Independent NDVI corroborates the spatial pattern of the six-indicator index: Pearson $r=0.705$ and Spearman $\rho=0.737$ over 1,785 valid points. With NDVI as the target, precipitation leads the stratified associations ($q=0.626$), ahead of barren/degraded (0.548), grassland (0.378) and temperature (0.178). Terrain regression gives $R^2=0.059$ for NDVI. In time, the regional NDVI level correlation with the reconstructed regional index is $-0.17$. Its trend changes sign between the all-clear-pixel and common-mask summaries, and the VIIRS-only level correlations also depend on the mask. Thus the independent product supplies spatial consistency for the point index, while the temporal warning remains conditional on the regional index's construction.

\subsection{Obstacle factors}
\label{sec:obstacle}
The obstacle degree describes the weighted deficiency of each primary-index component \citep{jing2024obstacle}. For normalized input $x'_{ij}$ and the same combined weight $w_j$ used in the eight-indicator ESI, $O_{ij}=(1-x'_{ij})w_j$ and $O^{*}_{ij}=O_{ij}/\sum_j O_{ij}$. Annual degrees sum to one, and phase values are their arithmetic means. The 2005--2010 and 2011--2023 division follows the index peak as a descriptive reference. A Pettitt test gives $p=0.148$, without a statistically identified break.

Under the primary weights, the leading 2005--2010 deficiencies are water (0.275), forest (0.264) and grassland (0.132). Over 2011--2023 they are grassland change rate (0.369), cropland (0.212) and water (0.103) (Fig.~\ref{fig:obstacle}). These values are calculated directly from the primary normalized matrix and weights. Alternative entropy, subjective and equal weights change their magnitudes and some rankings (Table~\ref{tab:obstacle-scheme}). The diagnosis therefore identifies weighted deficiencies of the specified index.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
\subsection{What the regional index measures}
The primary index describes relative ecological-security conditions within a fixed climate and land-cover reference. Its 2010 peak and the following mild-warning grades persist after removing the redundant ecological-land indicator, but the trend changes with the indicator set. The grassland-change-rate contribution helps explain the peak, and its dependence on interpolation links the temporal narrative to the way sparse land-cover observations are reconstructed. This distinction matters in alpine landscapes, where vegetation, water services and management pressures operate at different spatial and temporal scales \citep{qilian_ez_2024,westliaohe_esp_2026,china_ecoenv_2024}.

Spatial consistency supplies a complementary result. The six-indicator point index agrees with NDVI across the region, and precipitation becomes the leading factor when NDVI replaces the constructed target. The temporal NDVI comparisons are different, emphasizing that agreement across places and agreement through years answer different questions. Monitoring programs can use this separation to choose observations appropriate to each intended inference.

\subsection{Comparison with previous assessments}
The reserve-scale framework of Liu et al.\ combines DPSIR organization, combined weights, grey forecasting and GeoDetector \citep{liu2023integrated}. Related mountain and reservoir assessments emphasize composite-index patterns \citep{changbai_dpsirm_2024,dpsir_dea_threegorges2023}. The present analysis adds explicit sensitivity to redundant indicators and the weighting ratio, time-origin comparisons with simple forecast benchmarks, and separate definitions for regional and spatial targets (Table~\ref{tab:compare}). The short non-monotonic series supports differing extrapolations across grey and ARIMA models. NGBM supplies a nonlinear response \citep{althobaiti_ngbm2022}, but the naive benchmark and GM rolling errors show why added model complexity needs a common retrospective comparison.

\subsection{Implications for monitoring and management}
\label{sec:management}
The warning bands can organize monitoring intensity within the park's existing functional plan. A higher relative deficiency can prompt examination of the underlying land-cover and climate observations before choosing a management action. The primary plateau-period diagnosis places grassland change rate and cropland first and second, with means 0.369 and 0.212, making grassland dynamics and cropland change useful candidates for closer observation. In arid western landscapes, water-resource and desertification monitoring can distinguish natural low vegetation cover from degradation requiring restoration.

Regional grades, county-window contrasts and point-scale maps have distinct supports. The county ordering changes with the sampled window and with area weighting, so the stable extremes provide regional context rather than a basis for county intervention ranks. A management trigger would additionally require independently observed ecological outcomes at the management-unit scale, assessment of false alarms and missed events, and an appropriate cost of intervention. The grade--response relationship is therefore proposed as a monitoring design to evaluate, with the continuous index and conditional projection spread supplied alongside the grade.

\subsection{Scope and future evaluation}
The evidence concerns reconstructed regional conditions and a separate spatial composite. Five land-cover epochs support annual interpolation, and derived rates depend on the interpolation scheme. Retrospective forecast errors condition on that completed index, while the bootstrap envelope conditions on its residual resampling assumptions. Spatial translation diagnostics assume periodic boundaries, and the correlation-variance approximation depends on lag support. Future evaluation can use an independently observed annual target at matching spatial support and data available at each prediction origin. Such evaluation would establish the connection between relative warning grades and ecological outcomes.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
Open climate data and five land-cover epochs yield a reconstructed eight-indicator ESI that peaks at 0.676 in 2010 and reaches 0.478 in 2023. Mild warning occurs in 16 of the 19 years. Grade agreement after removing the redundant ecological-land indicator coexists with substantial sensitivity to the derived grassland-change-rate term and other indicator choices. The conventional and Hamed--Rao full-period trend tests have $p=0.184$ and 0.193, while trend-free pre-whitening gives 0.012, so the full-period decline remains dependent on the variance convention.

Under the corrected consecutive-time response functions, the 2028 GM, NGBM and ARIMA point projections are 0.448, 0.428 and 0.485. Their rolling-five-year-error-weighted mixture is 0.459, with a central 95\% conditional bootstrap envelope of 0.249--0.779. These results summarize model and residual-process choices for the reconstructed index. Spatially, a distinct six-indicator index agrees with independent NDVI ($r=0.705$), but the annual regional series has a different relationship with greenness. The contribution is a relative assessment with explicit indicator, forecast and spatial-support conditions. Monitoring applications can evaluate those conditions against independent ecological outcomes before assigning intervention triggers.
