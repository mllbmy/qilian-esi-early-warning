\title{Ecological security early warning in the Qilian Mountain region, China: temporal dynamics, forecasts, and spatial associations}

\begin{abstract}
Nature reserves are critical for biodiversity conservation, yet their management often lacks quantitative early-warning tools that translate monitoring data into actionable signals. We assess and forecast ecological security (ES) in the Qilian Mountain region (including the Qilian Mountain National Park), China, over 2005--2023, building on a Driving--Pressure--State--Impact--Response (DPSIR) indicator system and combining subjective--objective weighting, three forecasting models (GM(1,1), the nonlinear grey Bernoulli model and ARIMA), and GeoDetector association analysis, using only open climate (CRU TS 4.10) and land-cover (CLCD) data. The ecological security index (ESI) rose to a peak of 0.676 in 2010 (relatively safe), then declined and stabilized within the mild-warning band, reaching 0.478 in 2023; 16 of 19 years were in the mild-warning grade, and this 19-year grade sequence is identical in the nine-indicator baseline (peak 0.709 in 2010, 0.486 in 2023). The 2023 grade was invariant to the subjective--objective weighting ratio, and the eight-indicator specification widens the interval over which no year changes grade from $\alpha=0.3$--0.4 to $\alpha=0.4$--0.8. The indicator system contains three exact identities, including an ecological-land proportion that duplicates grassland $+$ forest $+$ water with zero residual; deleting that duplicate column leaves all 19 warning grades unchanged, and the eight-indicator set is therefore adopted as the primary specification. Because the grades are relative to a study-specific normalisation range and to the indicator set, they are reported as relative grades accompanied by the continuous ESI and its uncertainty rather than as stand-alone management triggers. Three models are carried into 2024--2028 under a no-intervention scenario and they do not agree: GM(1,1) declines slowly from 0.469 to 0.448, the nonlinear grey Bernoulli model (NGBM) falls from 0.424 to 0.380, and ARIMA(0,1,1) stays at 0.485; their equal-weight mixture gives 0.438 in 2028. The 2028 grade therefore depends on the model (mild warning for GM(1,1), ARIMA and the mixture, medium warning for the NGBM), and every margin to the 0.400 boundary (+0.048, $-0.020$, +0.085 and +0.038) is of the same order as the rounding unit of the reported value, so the 2028 grade is not decision-relevant on its own. Bootstrap resampling (2,000 replicates) gives 2028 intervals of 0.400--0.508 and 0.302--0.697 for GM(1,1), 0.318--0.458 and 0.172--0.615 for the NGBM, and 0.166--0.945 for the equal-weight mixture, whose upper bound is distorted because the ARIMA interval itself reaches 1.113, above the $[0,1]$ support of a min--max normalised index; the 2028 probability of medium warning is 0.54 for the NGBM and 0.43 for the mixture. The accuracy of the three models is comparable and their ranking depends on the validation scheme (single holdout over 2019--2023: RMSE 0.0687 NGBM, 0.0652 GM(1,1), 0.0400 ARIMA; rolling-origin and leave-one-out schemes reorder them), so no single model is claimed to be most accurate; the NGBM power exponent selected on the training period alone is 0.15, while the full-series optimum is 0.05, at the lower bound of the search grid, and the exponent that minimises the 2019--2023 holdout error is also 0.05, so whether the 2028 projection crosses the 0.400 boundary depends on the exponent-selection window. Spatially, the barren/degraded class ($q=0.80$) and the grassland class ($q=0.71$) show the strongest stratified associations with the point-scale ESI, followed by precipitation ($q=0.51$) and temperature ($q=0.17$) (all $p \le 0.001$); all four are themselves components of the ESI, so these values contain a mechanical-correlation component, and a leave-one-target sensitivity, in which the factor under test is removed from the target, reduces the grassland $q$ by 48\% (to 0.37) and reverses the ordering of the two weakest factors; the strictly exogenous terrain factors explain only about 8\% of the point-scale variance (7.6--10.5\% for elevation and 2.4--6.5\% for slope across the six stratification schemes, joint overlay $q=0.166$); against an independent growing-season NDVI target the same factors explain 5.9\%. We propose management zoning aligned with the functional zoning of the Qilian Mountain National Park together with a grade--response table, and a pipeline transferable to other reserves whose analysis scripts, processed result tables, the ten figure files, and documented Stage~0--7 run order are openly available.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{Background}
Global biodiversity is declining at an unprecedented rate: vertebrate population sizes have fallen by an average of 68\% since 1970, terrestrial mammal biomass has decreased by at least 85\% since the late Pleistocene, and illegal wildlife trade is valued at up to USD 23 billion per year \citep{wwf2020living,ipcc2014climate}. Habitat loss, climate change, and poaching jointly threaten ecosystem stability. Protected areas (PAs) have become the cornerstone of \emph{in-situ} biodiversity conservation worldwide.

Yet the effectiveness of PAs is constrained by limited monitoring capacity. Conventional field surveys are labor-intensive and difficult to sustain over large or inaccessible areas; camera traps and remote sensing provide richer data but require systematic analytical frameworks to translate raw observations into management decisions \citep{kerry2022remote,bijl2022camera}. A recurring gap is the absence of \emph{early-warning} information: ecological degradation is often detected only after it has become severe, leaving little room for preventive intervention.

This monitoring-to-warning gap has been repeatedly identified by conservation initiatives. For example, international calls to reduce illegal wildlife trade have proposed cloud-based monitoring platforms that combine sensor networks, remote sensing, and artificial intelligence to deliver real-time alerts for habitat threats \citep{wwf2020living,kerry2022remote}; the early-warning decision module of such platforms typically rests on composite ecological-security indicators that compress multidimensional environmental information into an actionable signal. We formalize and empirically validate this warning step with real data.

An ecological security (ES) perspective offers such a systematic formalization. ES assessment compresses multidimensional environmental, social, and economic information into a composite index that can be tracked through time and space \citep{liu2023integrated,jinan_land_esp_2022}. We adopt this perspective and apply it to a national park where habitat integrity directly underpins wildlife conservation outcomes.

\subsection{Research status and knowledge gaps}
The pressure--state--response (PSR) and DPSIR frameworks are widely used to organize ES indicator systems \citep{changbai_dpsirm_2024,dpsir_dea_threegorges2023}. ES assessments at mountain and reserve scale include the Changbai Mountain region, Yunnan's underdeveloped areas, and the adjacent Lanzhou--Xining urban agglomeration \citep{lu2014changbai,ou2017yunnan,tong2020lanzhou}. Subjective weighting (AHP) \citep{saaty1980analytic}, objective weighting (entropy method), and their combinations are mainstream. Grey models (GM(1,1)) \citep{deng1982control} are favored for small-sample ES time series; Liu et al.\ integrated DPSIR, combined weights, GM(1,1), and GeoDetector into a reserve-scale early-warning framework \citep{liu2023integrated}. The GeoDetector method \citep{wang2016measure,wang2012environmental} quantifies the strength of spatial association between individual factors and ES heterogeneity \citep{westliaohe_esp_2026,china_ecoenv_2024,qilian_ez_2024,zhang2019geodetector}.

Knowledge gaps remain: (1) reserve-scale studies usually apply the DPSIR--GM(1,1)--GeoDetector components in isolation or with a single weighting scheme; (2) the sensitivity of ES results to the weighting ratio $\alpha$ is rarely tested; (3) the internal independence of the indicator system is seldom checked, although composite indices routinely contain duplicated or derived columns; (4) forecast accuracy is usually reported for a single validation split, and the probability that a forecast falls in each warning grade is rarely quantified; (5) the spatially stratified factors are often themselves components of the target index, so part of that association is mechanical; and (6) few studies translate early-warning results into management zoning.

\subsection{Objectives of this study}
This study aims to (1) quantify the 2005--2023 dynamics and warning state of ecological security in the Qilian Mountain region from open data; (2) test the robustness of the ESI and its warning grades to the subjective--objective weighting ratio ($\alpha = 0$--$1$) and to the grade thresholds; (3) test the internal independence of the indicator system and remove exactly duplicated indicators; (4) compare three forecasting models under five validation schemes, quantify the probability of each warning grade in the projection, and produce a 2024--2028 forecast; (5) quantify the spatially stratified associations between candidate factors and the point-scale ESI while separating the mechanical component contributed by factors that are themselves index inputs; and (6) derive management zoning aligned with the functional zoning of the Qilian Mountain National Park.

\section{Study area and data}
\label{sec:study}

\subsection{Study area}
The Qilian Mountain region, centered on the Qilian Mountain National Park (QMNP), is located in the northeastern margin of the Qinghai--Tibet Plateau, spanning the border of Gansu and Qinghai provinces (Fig.~\ref{fig:area}). The park protects typical alpine ecosystems, including glaciers, snowfields, alpine meadows, and desert steppes, which provide critical water services for the Hexi Corridor and inland river basins \citep{gao2023water}. We analyze a rectangular region ($36.0$--$40.5^{\circ}$N, $93.5$--$103.5^{\circ}$E) centered on the park and its buffer counties; conclusions are interpreted at the regional scale. The study region has an annual mean temperature of 0.8--2.0\,$^{\circ}$C and annual precipitation of 167--284\,mm over 2005--2023. Analysis units include the whole region and 11 counties (Tianzhu, Sunan, Subei, Aksai, Minle, Shandan, Menyuan, Qilian, Gangcha, Delingha, Tianjun).

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{DPSIR framework and indicator system}
\label{sec:dpsir}
Following the DPSIR framework \citep{changbai_dpsirm_2024,dpsir_dea_threegorges2023}, nine indicators cover the criteria layers: Driving forces (precipitation, temperature), Pressure (barren/degraded proportion, cropland proportion), State (grassland, forest, water proportions), Impact (grassland change rate), and Response (ecological land proportion). The set relies exclusively on open, reproducible data (CRU TS 4.10 climate; CLCD 30\,m land cover) and captures both the climate forcing and the land-surface response that dominate ecological security in arid--semiarid alpine ecosystems. Temperature is assigned a positive direction because heat limits vegetation growth in this cold setting; reversing the direction and dropping the indicator entirely are both reported as sensitivities (Sec.~\ref{sec:tempdir}). Table~\ref{tab:indicators} lists all indicators with their directions. Fig.~\ref{fig:framework} outlines how the DPSIR causal chain connects to the ESI-based warning pipeline; all indicators are min--max normalized to $[0,1]$ before weighting (Sec.~\ref{sec:esi}).

Three exact linear identities exist within this set and were verified against the source tables at full precision (Sec.~\ref{sec:deredundancy}). (i) The Response-layer ecological land proportion equals grassland $+$ forest $+$ water, with a residual of exactly zero: it is the same column under two names. (ii) The Impact-layer grassland change rate is the annual rate of change of the State-layer grassland proportion, i.e. a derivative of a state variable, and its 2005 value is a convention of the differencing step rather than an observation. (iii) The Pressure-layer barren/degraded proportion equals $100-$ grassland $-$ forest $-$ water $-$ cropland, also with a zero residual, and correlates with the grassland proportion at $r=-0.92$; because the interannual ranges of forest, water and cropland are small, this column is close to a rescaled copy of the grassland proportion. The first identity duplicates information exactly, so the eight-indicator set obtained by deleting the ecological land proportion is adopted as the primary specification; the derivative and the complement are retained in the primary set for comparability with the DPSIR structure and are examined as sensitivities. At the point scale the barren/degraded indicator is the complement $1-$grassland$-$forest$-$water of a land-cover table without a cropland column, so 81 of the 1{,}914 samples that are dominated by cropland (75) or snow/ice (6) are counted as barren/degraded (Sec.~\ref{sec:spatial}).

\subsection{Combined weighting (AHP and entropy)}
Entropy weights are $e_j=-\frac{1}{\ln n}\sum_i p_{ij}\ln p_{ij}$ with $p_{ij}=x'_{ij}/\sum_i x'_{ij}$ and $w^{E}_j=(1-e_j)/\sum_j(1-e_j)$. AHP weights $w^{A}_j$ follow the principal-eigenvector method \citep{saaty1980analytic}. Because ecological security is most directly indicated by the State and Response layers, the criterion-level pairwise comparison matrix (five criteria on the Saaty 1--9 scale; Table~\ref{tab:ahp}) was derived from the DPSIR hierarchical priorities (State $>$ Response $>$ Impact $>$ Driving $=$ Pressure). This yields $\lambda_{\max}=5.015$, $CI=(\lambda_{\max}-n)/(n-1)=0.0038$ and $CR=CI/RI=0.0034<0.1$ ($RI=1.12$ at $n=5$), satisfying the consistency requirement. Within each criterion, indicators share the criterion weight equally. The combined weight is
\begin{equation}
w_j=\alpha\,w^{A}_j+(1-\alpha)\,w^{E}_j,\qquad \alpha\in[0,1],
\label{eq:weight}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha=0.4$ baseline and a full sensitivity analysis over $\alpha=0$--$1$ to test robustness to the subjective--objective balance. Entropy weights measure the interannual dispersion of an indicator rather than its ecological importance, which is consequential here because the grassland change rate is a difference of an interpolated series: its entropy weight ranges from 0.117 to 0.245 across interpolation schemes (linear, cubic spline, PCHIP, and the five observation epochs taken alone), and from 0.127 to 0.260 for the eight-indicator set, whereas no other indicator's entropy weight varies by more than 0.06 (Sec.~\ref{sec:weights}).

\subsection{Ecological security index (ESI)}
\label{sec:esi}
Indicators are first min--max normalized into $[0,1]$:
\begin{equation}
x'_{ij}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{x_{ij}-\min_j}{\max_j-\min_j}, & \text{positive},\\[2mm]
\dfrac{\max_j-x_{ij}}{\max_j-\min_j}, & \text{negative},\end{cases}
\label{eq:norm}
\end{equation}
and the ESI is the weighted sum
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{ESI}_i=\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_j\,x'_{ij},\qquad \mathrm{ESI}\in[0,1].
\label{eq:esi}
\end{equation}
Because the weights are time-invariant, the change between two years decomposes exactly into per-indicator contributions, $\Delta\mathrm{ESI}=\sum_j w_j\,\Delta x'_j$, with no weight-change term; this decomposition is used to interpret the 2005 minimum and the 2010 peak (Sec.~\ref{sec:tempdir}).

\subsection{Prediction models and warning classification}
\label{sec:warning}
The GM(1,1) model \citep{deng1982control} is built on the first-order accumulated series with least-squares estimates $(\hat a,\hat b)$:
\begin{equation}
\hat x^{(0)}(k+1)=\left(1-e^{\hat a}\right)\left(x^{(0)}(1)-\frac{\hat b}{\hat a}\right)e^{-\hat a k}.
\label{eq:gm}
\end{equation}
GM(1,1) assumes a monotonic exponential trend, which the observed ESI series violates (it rises to a peak in 2010 before declining); we therefore additionally apply the nonlinear grey Bernoulli model (NGBM), which generalizes GM(1,1) with a power exponent $\gamma$: $x^{(0)}(k)+a z^{(1)}(k)=b[z^{(1)}(k)]^{\gamma}$, and compare both with ARIMA. Model quality is evaluated by the posterior-variance ratio $C=S_2/S_1$ and the small-error probability $P$; grades: excellent ($C<0.35$, $P>0.95$), qualified ($C<0.50$, $P>0.80$), barely qualified ($C<0.65$, $P>0.70$).

Accuracy is assessed under five validation schemes, all scored on the ESI scale: a single holdout (fit 2005--2018, test 2019--2023); rolling-origin one-step forecasts with origins from 2013 and from 2015; rolling-origin five-step forecasts with origins from 2013; and leave-one-year-out cross-validation over the 19 years, in which each year is predicted from the remaining 18. The NGBM power exponent $\gamma$ is selected by grid search over 0.05--1.95 in steps of 0.05. Selecting $\gamma$ on the evaluation period makes a holdout result optimistic, so the leakage-safe variants are reported separately: when $\gamma$ is chosen on the training part alone (2005--2018) it takes the value 0.25 under a residual-sum-of-squares objective and 0.20 under a mean-absolute-error objective, and no training-only objective selects a value below 0.20. The projection itself starts from the 2023 observation, that is, at a forecast origin where the complete 2005--2023 record is available; the exponent used for the projection (0.20) is therefore selected on data that precede the projection, which is a different situation from a validation split inside the record. A second and weaker channel is that the ESI's min--max normalisation ranges and its entropy weights are estimated over the full period; a strictly leakage-free variant that re-estimates both inside each training window is reported as a sensitivity. Prediction intervals are obtained by residual bootstrap (2,000 replicates, fixed seed) under two uncertainty scopes---parameter uncertainty alone, and parameter uncertainty plus accumulated innovation uncertainty resampled independently or in moving blocks of three---and the bootstrap replicates are also used to compute, for each projection year, the probability of falling in each warning grade.

Forecasts and observed ESI values are interpreted against five warning levels: safe $[0.8,1]$, relatively safe $[0.6,0.8)$, mild warning $[0.4,0.6)$, medium warning $[0.2,0.4)$, and severe warning $[0,0.2)$. Thresholds are set at equal 0.2 intervals across the normalized range $[0,1]$, consistent with the five-grade warning convention used in DPSIR-based ecological-security early-warning frameworks for Chinese nature reserves \citep{liu2023integrated}; equal spacing keeps grades comparable across studies and avoids sample-dependent breakpoints. Because the boundaries are conventions on a relative index, grades are reported together with the continuous ESI, the grade-transition counts under $\alpha$ and threshold perturbations, and the bootstrap grade probabilities, and are not treated as a stand-alone trigger for automatic management action. The \emph{no-intervention scenario} used below means that the 2024--2028 projection continues the fitted 2005--2023 process without any additional land-cover or climate intervention: no new restoration or grazing-withdrawal measure is introduced after 2023, the five-epoch land-cover trajectory is extrapolated only through the model rather than through additional observed epochs, and no management feedback from the warning output to the driving forces is assumed.

\subsection{Spatially stratified association analysis (GeoDetector)}
Spatially stratified association analysis takes the 2020 point-scale ESI at 1{,}914 grid samples in the Qilian Mountain region as its target. The candidate factors are four---precipitation, temperature, grassland proportion, and barren/degraded proportion---each discretised into four equal-sample strata by sample quantiles, and the $q$ statistic measures the share of the spatial variance of the ESI accounted for by that stratification. Because all four factors are themselves components of the ESI, their baseline $q$ values contain a mechanical-correlation component shared with the target, and they are reported as associations with the point-scale ESI rather than as drivers of it. Three lines of evidence separate that component. First, a leave-one-target sensitivity removes the factor under test from the ESI components, re-normalises the survivors, re-weights them by entropy and by AHP over the surviving criteria layers, and recombines them at $\alpha=0.4$ to form a new target against which that factor is re-evaluated with the same stratification and 999 permutations; this is reported as a sensitivity analysis, not as attribution evidence. Second, the same test is repeated on a de-redundant point-scale target that also drops the complement-based barren indicator. Third, each $q$ is compared with a permutation null in which the target is held fixed and the factor is permuted, which bounds the share of the statistic that a chance association with the target's components would produce. The ``barren/degraded'' factor is not the same column as the ESI input \texttt{barren\_neg}: the two disagree at 81 of the 1{,}914 points (75 cropland and 6 snow/ice points). Stratification, sampling, and spatial-weight sensitivity are quantified in Sec.~\ref{sec:spatial}: the number of strata (quartile, quintile, Jenks natural breaks, and equal interval), the sampling scheme (full sample, 200 bootstrap resamples, and 200 random subsamples of 1{,}500 and 1{,}000 points), the 1{,}914-point sample drawn on a regular 0.15$^\circ$ grid, and the spatial weight matrix. The forest class is excluded from the main detection because only 15 of the 1{,}914 samples are forest-dominated, which makes quartile stratification degenerate to a single stratum and returns $q\equiv 0$; its association is instead estimated with a binary dominant-class stratification and with a zero-inflated stratification of the fractional cover, and is reported with bootstrap intervals. Interaction detection is likewise reported against a permutation null, because the joint statistic is inflated when factors and target share components.
\begin{equation}
q=1-\frac{\sum_{h=1}^{L}N_h\sigma_h^2}{N\sigma^2},
\label{eq:q}
\end{equation}
where $q\in[0,1]$, with larger values indicating a stronger spatial association with the ESI; $L$ is the number of strata, $N_h$ and $\sigma_h^2$ are the sample size and variance of stratum $h$, and $N$ and $\sigma^2$ are the total sample size and total variance of the ESI. Factor significance is tested by a permutation test (999 iterations) \citep{wang2016measure,wang2012environmental}, so $p=0.001$ is the resolution limit of that test; the interaction detector quantifies joint effects of factor pairs and is compared with a permutation null of 999 permutations (the 2{,}000 replicates are the bootstrap used for the confidence intervals), because two factors that are both components of the target produce an enhancing interaction mechanically. At the grid scale, land-cover factors are represented by the dominant CLCD class within a $\pm$0.02$^{\circ}$ neighbourhood (binary categorical factors), whereas the county- and region-level indicators are computed as area fractions; being mutually exclusive categories of a single classification, these factors are not statistically independent by construction, and when expressed as window fractions the grassland and barren/degraded proportions correlate at $r=-0.88$. Factors were sampled at 1{,}914 regular 0.15$^{\circ}$ grid points from the 2020 reference epoch of the CLCD series (consistent with the static county ESI) together with the 2005--2023 mean climate; temporal dynamics are quantified separately by the 19-year ESI series.

\subsection{Indicator weights}
\label{sec:weights}
Entropy weighting (Table~\ref{tab:weights}) identifies the grassland change rate ($w^{E}=0.260$) and the cropland proportion ($w^{E}=0.182$) as the most informative signals of the eight-indicator primary set, and the AHP criterion weights emphasize the three State indicators (grassland, forest and water; 0.195 each) and the Impact-layer grassland change rate (0.198). Removing the exactly duplicated ecological land column redistributes its weight by two rules that are stated here because they are not symmetric. The entropy weights are \emph{not} recomputed: because entropy weighting depends only on the normalised matrix and on column totals, deleting one column changes every surviving weight through the common denominator $1-w^{E}_{\mathrm{X9}}$ alone, so all eight weights are multiplied by the same factor $1.062653$ (the eight ratios span $2\times10^{-16}$), and this closed form reproduces a numerical recomputation to $3\times10^{-8}$. The same device is \emph{not} available for the AHP weights, whose five live criteria sum to 0.749769, so retaining the original values would break $\sum_j w_j=1$. Rule~A therefore renormalises the surviving criterion weights (Driving, Pressure, State, Impact) back to one, which multiplies every surviving AHP weight by $1.333744$; re-solving the $4\times4$ surviving judgement matrix instead (rule~D) gives $\lambda_{\max}=4.004$ with CR $=0.0015$ and differs from rule~A by at most 0.0122, in the State layer. Under rule~A the four live layers carry 15.4\% (Driving), 19.0\% (Pressure), 42.1\% (State) and 23.5\% (Impact) of the combined weight and sum to 1.000000, and the overlap group that motivated the de-duplication (State plus Impact) carries 65.6\%, against 69.3\% for the same group while the duplicated Response column was still present. The combined weights ($\alpha=0.4$) rank the grassland change rate (0.235), the water proportion (0.151), the forest proportion (0.144) and the cropland proportion (0.131) highest, so grassland dynamics, water availability and forest extent account for the largest shares of the weighted temporal variation of the ESI (Fig.~\ref{fig:weights}). The high entropy weight of the grassland change rate is largely an artefact of how the land-cover series is interpolated: it falls from 0.260 under linear interpolation to 0.246 under a cubic spline, 0.184 under PCHIP and 0.127 when only the five observation epochs are used, whereas no other indicator changes by more than 0.06 across the same four schemes (\ref{app:interp}). Entropy weighting responds to interannual variability, and a differenced interpolated series is the most variable column in the system; the weight therefore does not measure the ecological importance of grassland dynamics, and it is reported alongside the scheme it came from.

\subsection{ESI time series and warning grades (2005--2023)}
\label{sec:esi-series}
Under the primary eight-indicator specification (the duplicated ecological-land column removed; Sec.~\ref{sec:deredundancy}) the regional ESI rose from 0.289 (medium warning) in 2005 to a peak of 0.676 (relatively safe) in 2010, then declined and fluctuated within 0.45--0.58 (mild warning), reaching 0.478 in 2023. The nine-indicator series that retains the duplicated column runs 0.258 (2005) $\rightarrow$ 0.709 (2010) $\rightarrow$ 0.486 (2023) and is retained as the comparison specification. The two are correlated at $r=0.94$ (Spearman $\rho=0.80$), differ by at most 0.058 (mean absolute difference 0.034), and assign the same warning grade in all 19 years, so the grade distribution is 1 medium-warning year (2005), 2 relatively-safe years (2009, 2010), and 16 mild-warning years under either specification (Fig.~\ref{fig:esi}).

Trend testing is reported under three variance conventions and three indicator specifications. Under the eight-indicator primary specification no trend is significant, over the full record or over the post-peak segment: the conventional Mann--Kendall test gives $Z=-1.33$ ($p=0.184$) for 2005--2023 and $Z=-1.31$ ($p=0.189$) for 2010--2023, the Hamed--Rao correction gives $p=0.193$ and $p=0.185$, and both Sen slopes are small with confidence intervals that span zero ($-0.0031$\,yr$^{-1}$, 95\% CI $-0.0078$ to $+0.0017$; and $-0.0035$\,yr$^{-1}$, CI $-0.0117$ to $+0.0013$). Only trend-free pre-whitening returns a significant full-period decline ($p=0.012$) while returning a clearly non-significant segment ($p=0.200$), so the three conventions disagree and the modest decline is reported as suggestive rather than established. The nine-indicator baseline behaves differently and is retained as a contrast: it also shows no significant full-period trend ($Z=-1.12$, $p=0.263$; Sen $-0.0032$\,yr$^{-1}$, CI $-0.0088$ to $+0.0034$), but its 2010--2023 segment is significant under all three conventions ($p=0.006$, 0.006 and 0.002; Sen $-0.0086$\,yr$^{-1}$, CI $-0.0153$ to $-0.0037$), with a Hamed--Rao effective sample size of only 0.40 of $n$, that correction being numerically unstable on records of this length. The post-2010 decline is therefore a property of the nine-indicator specification and does not survive removal of the duplicated ecological land column. A six-indicator specification that also drops the barren complement reverses the sign of the trend over the full record, so neither the sign nor the significance of the trend is specification-free.

Perturbing each of the four grade boundaries by $\pm$0.05 (eight scenarios) leaves the 2023 grade unchanged in every scenario. Under the primary specification only two boundaries matter: lowering the 0.6 boundary by 0.05 moves 2007 to relatively safe, raising it by 0.05 returns 2009 to mild warning, and raising the 0.4 boundary by 0.05 moves 2020 to medium warning; the 0.2 and 0.8 boundaries change no year. Under the nine-indicator specification the same perturbation changes 7 year-grades in total, with the largest single scenario changing 6 years (2007, 2011--2015). Both counts are small relative to a 19-year record but are not zero, which is why grades are reported together with the continuous ESI rather than as a stand-alone signal.

\subsection{Indicator independence and de-redundancy}
\label{sec:deredundancy}
The three identities of Sec.~\ref{sec:dpsir} were verified against the source tables at full precision. The Response-layer ecological land proportion equals grassland $+$ forest $+$ water with a maximum absolute residual of exactly 0 over the 19 years, and the Impact-layer grassland change rate equals $100\times$ the year-on-year change of the grassland proportion with the same zero residual, its 2005 value being set to zero by the differencing convention; the archived three-decimal indicator table reproduces both identities to within $5\times10^{-4}$, which is the rounding scale of that table. The Pressure-layer barren/degraded proportion also has a zero residual ($100-$ grassland $-$ forest $-$ water $-$ cropland), and correlates with the grassland proportion at $r=-0.93$ over the five observation epochs and $r=-0.92$ over the 19 interpolated years, because the interannual ranges of forest (0.14 percentage points), water (0.42) and cropland (0.15) are an order of magnitude smaller than that of grassland (1.67): the column is effectively a rescaled copy of grassland. Two further specification issues are quantitative rather than exact. First, the regional land-cover table has no snow/ice, impervious-surface or wetland column, so the complement-based barren/degraded term absorbs shrub and built-up cover, which together occupy 0.14--0.21\% of the region; the six available classes themselves close to 100\% (maximum absolute residual $5.8\times10^{-13}$). Second, at the point scale the barren/degraded indicator is the complement $1-$grassland$-$forest$-$water of a table without a cropland column: 1{,}122 of the 1{,}914 samples have zero grassland, forest and water and are therefore forced to barren/degraded $=1$, and 81 of those samples are dominated by cropland (75) or snow/ice (6). The complement is an identity only for the 792 samples with a positive grassland, forest or water fraction (maximum absolute residual 0). A county-level residual specification of the same kind (barren $=100-$ forest $-$ grassland $-$ water $-$ cropland) departs from the CLCD class-7 proportion by up to 2.01 percentage points, whereas the stored county barren values match the raster window to $10^{-4}$.

Table~\ref{tab:deredundancy} reports the nine-indicator baseline and five alternative specifications. Deleting the exactly duplicated ecological land proportion leaves all 19 warning grades unchanged, keeps the peak year at 2010, and shifts the ESI by at most 0.058 (mean absolute difference 0.034); it is adopted as the primary specification, and it also widens the range of $\alpha$ over which no grade changes (Sec.~\ref{sec:alpha}). Deleting the grassland change rate instead moves the peak to 2017 and changes 11 of 19 grades, deleting it together with the ecological land proportion moves the peak to 2019 and changes 8 grades, and a six-indicator set that drops the barren complement as well changes 6 grades; the substituted five-indicator set keeps the peak year at 2010 but changes 11 grades and moves the 2023 ESI to 0.305 (medium warning). Because the six-indicator series is essentially uncorrelated with the nine-indicator series in rank (Spearman $\rho=-0.02$), the temporal narrative of a 2010 peak followed by a mild-warning plateau is not independent of the choice of indicator set; in particular it requires either the grassland change rate or the derived ecological land and barren terms to be retained, so it is a property of the DPSIR specification adopted here rather than a specification-free finding. Retaining the grassland change rate is a defensible choice for a conservative specification comparable with other DPSIR studies, but its entropy weight is largely an artefact of interpolating the land-cover series (Sec.~\ref{sec:weights}), so it cannot be read as evidence that grassland dynamics matter most ecologically. The proportion of combined weight carried by the State, Impact and Response layers falls from 69.3\% to 65.6\% when the duplicated column is removed.

\subsection{Forecast comparison: GM(1,1), NGBM, and ARIMA}
\label{sec:forecast}
The three models give different 2024--2028 trajectories under the no-intervention scenario defined in Sec.~\ref{sec:method}, and none of them is claimed to be the most accurate, so all three are reported together with their equal-weight mixture (Table~\ref{tab:forecast}; Fig.~\ref{fig:esi}). GM(1,1) declines slowly from 0.469 to 0.448, the NGBM falls more steeply from 0.424 to 0.380, and ARIMA(0,1,1) is flat at 0.485; the equal-weight mixture of the three point forecasts gives 0.438 in 2028. The projected grade depends on the model: mild warning for GM(1,1) (+0.048 above the 0.400 boundary), for ARIMA (+0.085) and for the mixture (+0.038), and medium warning for the NGBM ($-0.020$). Each margin is small relative to the 0.104 spread between the three point forecasts, and is of the same order as the rounding unit of the reported value: at one decimal place the NGBM value becomes 0.4 and its grade flips back to mild warning, and shifting the 0.400 boundary by $\pm0.005$ to $\pm0.02$ flips the NGBM grade accordingly (with the boundary at 0.395 the bootstrap probability of a medium-warning outcome is 0.57, and at 0.405 it is 0.61). The 2028 grade therefore carries no decision weight on its own. Whether the NGBM crosses the boundary also depends on how its power exponent is chosen: the training-only residual-sum-of-squares optimum for the eight-indicator set is $\gamma=0.15$, the full-series optimum is $\gamma=0.05$ at the lower bound of the search grid, and $\gamma=0.05$ also minimises the 2019--2023 holdout error, so the two windows available at the projection origin select different exponents and give 2028 values of 0.420 and 0.380. One qualification applies to the wide intervals: the ARIMA interval reaches 1.113, above the $[0,1]$ support of a min--max normalised index, because accumulated innovations are added to the level without a support constraint, so the upper tail of the ARIMA and mixture intervals is not physically meaningful and is reported only for completeness.

Accuracy is comparable across the three models and their ordering depends on the validation scheme. The five-scheme comparison of Table~\ref{tab:validation} was computed on the nine-indicator calibration, the specification for which all five schemes exist, and it is reported as nine-indicator evidence: the leading model differs in every scheme (ARIMA in the holdout and in the rolling five-step forecasts, GM(1,1) in the rolling one-step forecasts from 2013 and in 15 of the 19 leave-one-out folds, the NGBM in the rolling one-step forecasts from 2015), so no single model is claimed to be most accurate and the projection is reported for all three. Two sources of optimism in the earlier single-split comparison are quantified rather than removed. First, an exponent that is not the training-period optimum is reachable only by allowing the evaluation period into the grid search: under the eight-indicator primary set the training-only residual-sum-of-squares optimum is $\gamma=0.15$ and the holdout error is instead minimised at $\gamma=0.05$, at the lower bound of the search grid, so the holdout column moves with the selection window and no training-only objective reproduces the archived training-period value. Second, the normalisation ranges and entropy weights are estimated over the full period; a strictly leakage-free variant of the nine-indicator calibration that re-estimates both inside each training window reverses the ordering entirely (NGBM 0.0473, ARIMA 0.0854, GM(1,1) 0.0991), so the three models differ less in genuine out-of-sample skill than the single-split numbers suggest. For the eight-indicator primary set the five schemes were not recomputed, which is stated here as a limitation rather than filled in from the nine-indicator run; the exponent of the projection is selected on the complete 2005--2023 record, which is legitimate at a forecast origin of end-2023 because the whole record precedes the projection.

Bootstrap resampling (2{,}000 replicates, with the NGBM exponent reselected inside each replicate) quantifies the projection uncertainty of the primary series (Table~\ref{tab:forecastunc}). For the NGBM the 95\% interval widens from 0.377--0.481 (2024) to 0.318--0.458 (2028) under parameter uncertainty alone, and to 0.172--0.615 under moving-block resampling that also accumulates innovations; with the exponent fixed at its training-period optimum of 0.15 the 2028 interval narrows to 0.337--0.437, and with the full-series optimum of 0.05 the point projection moves to 0.421. Under accumulated innovation uncertainty the 2028 grade probabilities of the NGBM are 0.54 medium warning, 0.38 mild warning, 0.05 severe warning and 0.03 relatively safe, so the modal grade is medium warning with a probability barely above one half. The alternative models diverge in level rather than in interval width: GM(1,1) declines only slowly, from 0.469 in 2024 to 0.448 in 2028 (slope $-0.005$ yr$^{-1}$, about one third of the NGBM slope), with a probability of mild warning of 0.53, whereas ARIMA(0,1,1) is constant at 0.485 with probabilities of 0.38 medium warning, 0.27 mild warning and 0.13 relatively safe. The equal-weight mixture of the three models gives 0.438 in 2028 with a probability of medium warning of 0.43. The projection is therefore one model-conditional trajectory within the reported interval, not a consensus forecast.

\subsection{Robustness to the weighting ratio $\alpha$}
\label{sec:alpha}
Sensitivity analysis over $\alpha=0$--$1$ shows that the 2023 ESI of the nine-indicator baseline increases from 0.425 (pure entropy, $\alpha=0$) to 0.578 (pure AHP, $\alpha=1$) (Fig.~\ref{fig:alpha}), while its warning grade remains mild warning throughout; under the eight-indicator primary specification the same endpoints are 0.415 and 0.573. The historical grade distribution is sensitive to $\alpha$: the number of mild-warning years falls from 16 at $\alpha=0.3$--$0.4$ to 5 at $\alpha\ge0.9$, and one severe-warning year appears from $\alpha\ge0.7$ (Table~\ref{tab:alpha}; the complete $11\times19$ matrix for both specifications is Table~\ref{tab:alpha-full} in \ref{app:alpha}). Under the eight-indicator primary specification, by contrast, the grade changes in no year for $\alpha$ between 0.4 and 0.8, in one year at $\alpha=0.9$ and in four years at $\alpha=1.0$, and the 2023 grade is mild warning at every $\alpha$ from 0 to 1. The duplicated ecological-land column is thus the main amplifier of the $\alpha$ sensitivity, because AHP gives that column a weight of 0.250 on its own. The baseline $\alpha=0.4$ lies in the widest stable interval under both specifications, so the current-state conclusion is robust to the subjective--objective balance; the historical grade distribution, however, is not, and is reported as $\alpha$-conditional.

\subsection{Temperature direction and the composition of the 2010 peak}
\label{sec:tempdir}
The positive direction assigned to temperature is a substantive choice, so both alternatives are reported. Reversing it to negative leaves the sign of the trend and the 2023 grade unchanged but lowers the index: the largest year-wise difference is 0.078 (mean 0.039), the rank correlation with the baseline is $\rho=0.85$ ($r=0.90$), three grades change, and the 2023 ESI becomes 0.422; dropping temperature changes one grade and gives 0.450. The 2010 peak survives all three treatments, so it does not depend on the treatment of temperature.

The exact decomposition of Sec.~\ref{sec:esi} identifies what generates the two ends of the record. Between 2005 and 2023 the nine-indicator ESI rises by 0.228, of which the State layer contributes $+0.270$ (118\% of the change), Response $+0.080$ (35\%), while Pressure contributes $-0.086$ ($-38\%$), Impact $-0.022$ ($-9\%$) and Driving $-0.014$ ($-6\%$); at the indicator level the largest single contributions are the forest proportion ($+0.120$, 53\%), the water proportion ($+0.112$, 49\%), the cropland proportion ($-0.119$, $-52\%$) and precipitation ($-0.043$, $-19\%$). The 2005 minimum is an observation epoch of the CLCD record rather than an interpolated endpoint (Table~\ref{tab:data}), and it is unchanged by the temperature-direction alternatives. The 2010 peak is likewise generated mainly by the grassland change rate, whose normalised value in 2010 is 0.98 of its range. The subsequent 2010--2023 decline ($-0.223$) is attributed by the same decomposition to the grassland change rate ($-0.150$, 67\%), the grassland proportion ($-0.060$, 27\%), precipitation ($-0.055$, 25\%), the cropland proportion ($-0.054$, 24\%) and the ecological land proportion ($-0.044$, 20\%), partly offset by the forest proportion ($+0.078$, $-35\%$ of a negative change) and the water proportion ($+0.066$); no single indicator accounts for more than about two thirds of the decline, and the largest term is the derived grassland change rate rather than an observed state variable. The variance decomposition of the point-scale ESI assigns 93\% of the variance share to the land-cover block and 7\% to the climate block, with the barren complement (0.42) and grassland (0.37) dominant at the indicator level and temperature carrying a negative share, which follows from its negative correlation with the index.

\subsection{County-level relative index}
\label{sec:county}
The static county index for 2020 uses the same combined weighting ($\alpha = 0.4$) as the regional analysis, with entropy weights recomputed from the 11-county cross-section and AHP weights set equal across the six county-level indicators available; the inputs are the 2020 land-cover proportions and the 2005--2023 climate means for a $\pm$0.1$^{\circ}$ centroid window, min--max normalised across the 11 units. It ranks Sunan (0.558) and Tianzhu (0.525) highest and Aksai (0.067) lowest (Table~\ref{tab:county}; Fig.~\ref{fig:spatial}). Because it is a cross-sectional index normalised across 11 units rather than in time, these are \emph{relative county indices}: they are not comparable with the regional ESI and cannot be read against the five warning thresholds. Three sources of uncertainty were quantified over 64 combinations of window half-width ($\pm$0.05$^{\circ}$ to $\pm$0.30$^{\circ}$), spatial weight (entropy, AHP, equal) and land-cover source (CLCD class 7, residual-barren formula, no-barren composition). The rank correlation with the baseline falls from 0.68 at $\pm$0.05$^{\circ}$ to 0.51 at $\pm$0.20$^{\circ}$ and 0.49 at $\pm$0.30$^{\circ}$, and 8 of 11 counties change rank between the two extreme windows. Across all 64 combinations the rank span is 7 places for Sunan, 7 for Gangcha and 9 for Delingha, and only two statements survive every combination: Tianzhu is always in the top three (rank 1--3) and Aksai is always in the bottom two (rank 10--11, last in the reference configuration). Substituting area-weighted polygon means for centroid windows moves Sunan from 1st to 9th and Delingha from 4th to 1st (Table~\ref{tab:countyrank}). Water is the most sensitive indicator: its entropy weight rises from 0.399 to 0.403 as the window widens from $\pm$0.20$^{\circ}$ to $\pm$0.30$^{\circ}$, and the water fraction of Gangcha changes from 0.05\% to 16.3\% to 24.2\% as the window narrows from $\pm$0.30$^{\circ}$ to $\pm$0.10$^{\circ}$ to $\pm$0.05$^{\circ}$, because the Qinghai Lake shoreline enters and leaves the window. Multi-year county indices cannot be computed from the data used here, because only the 2020 CLCD tile can be reprocessed alongside this repository and no county-boundary-consistent land-cover series was available for the other four epochs. County values are therefore used only as a coarse east--west contrast, and the management zoning of Sec.~\ref{sec:management} rests on the functional zoning of the national park together with the grade--response relationship rather than on county ranks.

\subsection{Spatially stratified associations}
\label{sec:spatial}
Baseline detection over the 1{,}914 points gives barren/degraded $q=0.802$, grassland $q=0.708$, precipitation $q=0.506$ and temperature $q=0.170$ (all $p=0.001$, the resolution limit of the 999-permutation test; Fig.~\ref{fig:qbar}). Because all four factors are components of the index they are tested against, part of each $q$ is mechanical, and three checks quantify how much. First, the leave-one-target sensitivity recomputes the target without the factor under test and returns $q=0.711$ (barren/degraded), 0.381 (precipitation), 0.366 (grassland) and 0.274 (temperature), all $p=0.001$; the largest single change is grassland, which falls by 48.2\%, while temperature rises by 61.3\% from a low base. Second, on a de-redundant point-scale target that also drops the complement-based barren term, grassland $q=0.540$, precipitation $q=0.486$ and temperature $q=0.117$, but the grassland $q$ against the removed factor collapses to $6.3\times10^{-5}$ ($p=0.73$), that is, to zero within permutation noise, which identifies the grassland association as largely a restatement of the barren complement. Third, a block-wise cross-detection evaluates each factor against the sub-index of its own DPSIR block (a target that contains the factor, hence mechanical) and against that of the other block (no shared component). The resulting mechanical share $1-q_{\mathrm{cross}}/q_{\mathrm{own}}$ is 0.42 for barren/degraded, 0.47 for precipitation, 0.57 for grassland, 0.74 for temperature, 0.98 for forest and 0.99 for water, so for the composition factors most of the apparent association is a property of index construction rather than an independent ecological signal. The strictly exogenous terrain factors, which belong to no block, give elevation $q=0.090$ and slope $q=0.065$ (both $p=0.001$), jointly 0.166 over 16 strata, and a regression of the point index on elevation and slope gives $R^2=0.081$; their partial correlations with the index fall to 0.025 and $-0.104$ once precipitation and temperature are controlled. Terrain therefore explains only about 8\% of the point-scale variance and cannot account for the spatial pattern on its own. The factors are accordingly reported as spatially stratified associations with the index rather than as its drivers.

The detection is stable to how the factors and samples are defined. Across six stratification schemes precipitation $q$ ranges from 0.506 to 0.541 and temperature from 0.159 to 0.196, whereas grassland and barren/degraded take only two distinct values in the sample and their $q$ is scheme-invariant; across 200 bootstrap resamples and 200 random subsamples of 1{,}500 and 1{,}000 points the factor ranking is unchanged in every replicate (Spearman $\rho=1.00$) and the 95\% intervals stay within $\pm$0.04 of the full-sample value for precipitation, temperature and the terrain factors, widening to $\pm$0.13 for grassland in the smallest subsample (Table~\ref{tab:strat}). The forest class is treated separately because only 15 of the 1{,}914 samples are forest-dominated: dominant-class stratification gives $q=0.065$ with a bootstrap 95\% interval of 0.034--0.099 (0.019--0.117 under a spatial block bootstrap), a presence/absence stratification gives 0.189 and a four-level zero-inflated stratification of the fractional cover gives 0.206, whereas the same dominant-class factor reaches 0.927 against a reference ecological-land target, which is an artefact of comparing a class with an index constructed from it. The interaction detector returns enhancing interactions for all six pairs, but none of the six reaches the non-linear criterion $q_{12}>q_1+q_2$; all are therefore classified as bivariate-enhancing under the weaker $q_{12}>\max(q_1,q_2)$ criterion used here, but the mechanical share of the joint statistic ranges from 0.20 (temperature$\times$barren) to 0.87 (grassland$\times$barren), the strongest apparent interactions being those between factors that already share composition with the target; the excess over the permutation null is 0.11--0.68 and the joint $q$ exceeds the larger single-factor $q$ in every one of 1{,}000 bootstrap replicates. The interaction results are therefore reported as a property of the composite index rather than as evidence of ecological coupling (Table~\ref{tab:interaction}; Fig.~\ref{fig:interaction}).

An independent target was assembled to test how much of this structure survives outside the index. A growing-season mean NDVI (six fixed dates, clear-sky pixels only) was extracted from the NOAA CDR AVHRR/VIIRS record at the same 1{,}914 points for 2020 and as a regional annual mean for 2005--2023; the product is not an input to the index. Spatially the agreement is strong: the point-scale index correlates with the independent NDVI at $r=0.705$ ($\rho=0.737$, $n=1{,}785$ points with valid retrievals), and the two-way stratified test is almost symmetric, NDVI strata explaining $q=0.548$ of the index variance and index strata $q=0.527$ of the NDVI variance. The attribution ranking is not transferable, however. With the independent NDVI as the target the order becomes precipitation (0.626) $>$ barren/degraded (0.548) $>$ grassland (0.378) $>$ temperature (0.178) $>$ slope (0.090) $>$ elevation (0.079), against barren/degraded (0.818) $>$ grassland (0.814) $>$ precipitation (0.548) $>$ temperature (0.197) on the index target: the stratified association of the grassland factor falls by 54\% and of the barren factor by 33\% when the target is independent, precipitation rises by 14\% and becomes the leading factor, and the rank correlation between the two orderings is 0.771. Terrain explains $R^2=0.059$ of the independent NDVI, close to its value of 0.081 for the index. The annual series do not co-vary, however: the regional growing-season NDVI rises by 0.0013 NDVI units per year over 2005--2023 under the all-clear-pixel mean, but falls by 0.0010 per year under a common clear-pixel mask, so even the sign of the regional trend is not robust while the index rises by 0.0006 per year, and their year-to-year level correlation is $-0.17$ over the full period ($-0.27$ in the VIIRS segment 2014--2023, where sensor continuity is better, and $+0.44$ in the VIIRS segment under a common clear-pixel mask (full period $-0.10$)), while the pointwise NDVI trend is uncorrelated with the static 2020 index ($r=-0.03$, $n=250$). The spatial pattern of the index is thus corroborated by an independent observation, whereas its interannual variation is not reproduced by regional greenness, which is consistent with the annual series being driven substantially by the differenced land-cover term rather than by vegetation greenness itself.

\subsection{Spatial clustering}
\label{sec:moran}
Moran's $I$ of the 2020 point index is 0.6431 with row-standardised $k=8$ nearest-neighbour weights on inverse distances, reproducing the archived value (0.643095) digit for digit. The statistic is insensitive to the weight definition: over $k=4$ to 50 and binary, inverse-distance and inverse-squared-distance weights it spans 0.575--0.666, every specification returns a permutation $p$ of 0.001 except the archived run, which records 0.005, and the normal approximation gives $z>40$ throughout (Table~\ref{tab:moran}). Most of the clustering is accounted for by the same composition that forms the index: regressing the point index on the six components ($R^2=0.85$) leaves a residual Moran's $I$ of 0.30 and on the four climate and terrain factors ($R^2=0.54$) one of 0.26, whereas regressing on terrain alone ($R^2=0.081$) still leaves 0.62. Spatial autocorrelation is therefore not treated as independent evidence of ecological connectivity.

\subsection{Obstacle factors}
\label{sec:obstacle}
Following the obstacle-degree diagnosis of composite ecological-security indices \citep{jing2024obstacle}, the deficiency of indicator $j$ in year $i$ is $D_{ij}=1-x'_{ij}$, its factor weight is $F_j=w_j$, the obstacle degree is $O_{ij}=D_{ij}F_j$, and the degrees are normalised across indicators within each year as $O^{*}_{ij}=O_{ij}/\sum_j O_{ij}$, so that the normalised values sum to 1 in every year. Phase values are arithmetic means of the annual normalised obstacle degrees. The phase division itself is descriptive: it is fixed by the 2010 peak of the index (Sec.~\ref{sec:esi-series}), because the Pettitt test places a change point at 2016 ($K=56$, $p=0.148$, not significant) and the largest difference in segment means occurs at 2008, so the record contains no statistically supported phase boundary, and the policy periods cited in the Introduction are background rather than a criterion.

Under the eight-indicator primary specification, the largest mean obstacles during the recovery phase (2005--2010) are the water proportion (0.274), the forest proportion (0.260) and the cropland proportion (0.127), and during the plateau phase (2011--2023) they are the grassland change rate (0.384), the cropland proportion (0.218) and the water proportion (0.099); the leading factor in individual years is the grassland change rate in 13 of 19 years (2011--2023) and the water proportion in the remaining 6 (2005--2010). These phase means are weight-conditional rather than weight-free: under the nine-indicator weight set used by the archived obstacle table the plateau-phase leader is the same factor (0.364) ahead of the cropland proportion (0.206), with entropy weights alone the leader is still the grassland change rate (0.368) and with equal weights it becomes the cropland proportion (0.218) while the grassland change rate falls to second, and with AHP weights alone the recovery-phase leader becomes the forest proportion (0.246). Because the obstacle degree is $O_{ij}=(1-x'_{ij})w_j$ normalised within each year, the ranking inherits the weighting and the interpolation scheme of Sec.~\ref{sec:weights} and is therefore reported as a weighted-deficiency ranking rather than as an intrinsic constraint ordering. The archived phase means of the nine-indicator obstacle table were produced by an implementation whose generating script is not part of the repository; an independent recomputation from a re-implemented index reproduces them to within 0.014, the largest differences falling on the derived grassland change rate (\ref{app:obstacle}).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Ecological security changes and their associations}
The 2005--2010 recovery of the index is consistent with large-scale ecological restoration (grazing withdrawal and revegetation programmes) and with wetter years, but the policy periods themselves cannot be verified from the data used here, and the 2010 peak is generated mainly by the derived grassland change rate rather than by an observed state variable (Sec.~\ref{sec:tempdir}). The subsequent mild-warning plateau reflects a barren-dominated land cover ($\sim$56\%) together with climate fluctuation. The high combined weight of the grassland change rate is consistent with the importance of alpine meadow ecosystems for regional ecological security, in line with findings that grassland degradation is a leading constraint in alpine regions \citep{qilian_ez_2024,westliaohe_esp_2026}; because that weight is partly an artefact of interpolating the land-cover series (Sec.~\ref{sec:weights}), the argument is made from the decomposition rather than from the weight alone. Both the coarse east--west contrast of the county index and the stratified associations indicate that precipitation is more strongly associated with the index than temperature (baseline $q=0.506$ vs.\ 0.170; leave-one-target 0.381 vs.\ 0.274), a pattern consistent with arid-zone studies in which moisture availability, rather than the thermal regime, limits ecosystem productivity \citep{china_ecoenv_2024}.

For wildlife and biodiversity conservation, the mild-warning state and the projected decline carry a concrete message: habitat integrity in the Qilian Mountain region is not improving, and monitoring programs that rely on static habitat assessment may miss slow degradation. The early-warning signal formalized here links raw environmental data to actionable warning grades, the decision-support input envisioned by recent wildlife-monitoring frameworks \citep{kerry2022remote,bijl2022camera}. The signal builds on the early-warning decision module of international wildlife-monitoring initiatives: composite ecological-security indicators convert remote-sensing and ground-monitoring data into actionable warning grades, closing the habitat monitoring--early-warning--response loop; we validate this framework empirically for the Qilian Mountain National Park.

\subsection{Comparison with previous studies}
Relative to the reserve-scale early-warning framework that combines DPSIR, combined weights, GM(1,1) and GeoDetector \citep{liu2023integrated}, our results confirm that this toolchain transfers to national-park regions, while extending it in the three respects summarized in Table~\ref{tab:compare}. First, robustness to the analyst's weighting choice is quantified instead of asserted: the current-state grade is mild warning at every $\alpha$ in 0--1, and the eight-indicator primary specification changes no grade over $\alpha=0.4$--0.8, but the historical grade distribution does depend on $\alpha$ (5 mild-warning years at $\alpha\ge0.9$ against 16 at $\alpha=0.4$), so the claim of $\alpha$-invariance applies to the current state only. Comparable DPSIR-based assessments report a single weighting scheme \citep{changbai_dpsirm_2024,dpsir_dea_threegorges2023}. Second, the observed series is non-monotonic and violates the monotonic-trend assumption of GM(1,1), yet the three models cannot be separated by a single accuracy claim: the leading model differs across five validation schemes, the exponent that produced the earlier holdout error cannot be selected without the evaluation period, and a strictly leakage-free variant reverses the ranking. A nonlinear grey variant remains a defensible choice for a short, non-stationary series \citep{althobaiti_ngbm2022}, but on this record it has not been shown to forecast better than GM(1,1) or ARIMA.

Third, linking the regional series to grid- and county-level associations reveals a scale dependence that single-scale studies cannot expose: the indicators dominating temporal variation (the grassland change rate and the cropland proportion; Table~\ref{tab:weights}) are not the factors with the highest spatially stratified association (the barren/degraded and grassland classes, both of which overlap the index inputs; Fig.~\ref{fig:qbar}). A fourth extension is diagnostic rather than descriptive: three exact identities in the indicator system, one of which duplicates a column, determine how much of the temporal narrative survives a change of indicator set (Table~\ref{tab:deredundancy}), and the mechanical component of every spatial association can be bounded by leave-one-target detection and block-wise cross-detection. Early-warning design should therefore pair temporal monitoring of grassland and cropland dynamics with spatially targeted control of land degradation, while reporting which part of each association is construction and which is ecology.

\subsection{Management implications}
\label{sec:management}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Zoning by the park's functional plan.} Because the grade boundaries are conventions on a relative index and the county ordering is not stable, zoning is proposed on the national park's own functional zones (core conservation, general control, traditional utilisation) crossed with the observed warning grade, rather than on a ranking of counties or grid cells. Under this scheme the mild-warning zones that have dominated since 2011 receive grassland restoration and grazing regulation within their existing permitted-use rules, while arid western units (Aksai, Delingha) receive desertification and water-resource monitoring rather than vegetation restoration \emph{per se}.
\item \emph{Grade--response table.} Each warning grade is paired with a pre-agreed action level: continuous monitoring and annual re-assessment at relatively safe; grazing and tourism load review at mild warning; restoration prioritisation and a documented cause analysis at medium warning; and mandatory intervention with a review of land-use approvals at severe warning. The continuous index value and its bootstrap interval accompany the grade, so that a unit whose 2028 interval spans two grades---as the 2028 regional projection does---is handled at the higher action level only when the interval lies predominantly in it.
\item \emph{Cropland regulation.} The cropland proportion is the second-ranked obstacle in the plateau phase (0.206 under combined weights, and the leading obstacle under equal weights), so expansion at the agropastoral ecotone should be controlled to curb land-use pressure.
\item \emph{What the thresholds are not.} The five bands are equal 0.2 intervals on a study-specific normalised range; they are neither ecological critical values nor a stand-alone trigger for automatic management response, and a margin between 0.02 and 0.09 for the three 2028 model projections and their mixture, all of the same order as the rounding unit of the reported values is explicitly not treated as a decision basis.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations and future work}
Several limitations bound these conclusions. (1) The indicator system contains exact internal identities: the ecological land proportion duplicates grassland $+$ forest $+$ water with a residual of exactly zero, the grassland change rate is the differenced grassland proportion with an arbitrary 2005 origin, and the barren/degraded proportion is the complement of the other land-cover columns (residual exactly zero, $r=-0.92$ with grassland). Deleting the exact duplicate leaves all 19 warning grades unchanged and is adopted as the primary specification, but the peak year and the number of grade changes move substantially under the other specifications (Table~\ref{tab:deredundancy}), so the historical narrative is specification-dependent. (2) The land-cover inputs have five epochs (2005, 2010, 2015, 2020, 2023) interpolated to 19 years, so 14 of the 19 years are interpolated; the entropy weight of the differenced grassland series ranges from 0.117 to 0.245 across interpolation schemes and therefore measures interannual variability rather than ecological importance. (3) The county index is static, cross-sectional and computed from $\pm$0.1$^{\circ}$ centroid windows: its ordering is window- and weight-dependent (rank correlation 0.49--0.68 against the baseline, 8 of 11 counties changing rank, area weighting moving Sunan from 1st to 9th), no multi-year county series can be built from the available epochs because only the 2020 CLCD tile can be reprocessed alongside this repository, and the values are not comparable with the regional thresholds, so county results are not used for prioritisation. (4) The spatially stratified associations are associations with a composite index and not attributions: four of the factors are index inputs whose mechanical share is 0.42--0.99, the point-scale barren/degraded term is a complement that counts 81 cropland- or snow-dominated samples as barren, and the strictly exogenous terrain factors explain only about 8\% of the point-scale variance. An independent target was assembled to break this circularity---a growing-season NDVI from the NOAA CDR AVHRR/VIIRS record extracted at the same 1{,}914 points, which is not an index input---and it corroborates the spatial pattern ($r=0.705$) while transferring the ranking (precipitation leads) and failing to reproduce the annual series (level correlation $-0.17$); being a different quantity on a different sensor record and spatial support, it serves as a consistency check and cannot validate the weighting, the thresholds or the DPSIR structure. (5) The forecast models cannot be ranked on this record: the leading model differs across five validation schemes, the exponent of the NGBM must be selected (training-only selection raises the holdout error from 0.034 to 0.069), the index normalisation and entropy weights are estimated over the full period so that a strictly leakage-free variant reverses the ranking, and the bootstrap intervals condition on one fitted model form and therefore exclude model-selection uncertainty, which the spread of the three 2028 projections of the primary series (0.380, 0.448, 0.485) makes explicit. (6) Two archived intermediate tables cannot be regenerated from the published scripts: the obstacle table, whose phase means are reproduced here to within 0.014 by an independent recomputation, and the archived forecast accuracy table whose NGBM entry required an exponent selected on the evaluation period. Both are identified in the repository documentation, are reported with their provenance stated, and are not the basis of any conclusion. (7) The point-scale weighting is concentrated: entropy weighting assigns $0.326$ to the forest proportion and $0.283$ to the water proportion, so 60.9\% of the weight rests on the 49 samples (2.6\% of 1{,}914) in which those two classes dominate, a direct consequence of the ``more concentrated is more informative'' property of entropy weighting; and the climate factors are sampled from 0.5$^{\circ}$ CRU grids onto 0.15$^{\circ}$ point samples, so their resampling intervals are optimistic and a grid-level clustered bootstrap would be needed to test them properly. (8) The warning thresholds are conventions on a relative index, the positive direction assigned to temperature is a choice whose reversal changes the index by up to 0.078, and the county- and grid-scale analyses inherit the resolution of the underlying products; future work should test the framework with a multi-year county land-cover series, an independent ecological target of the same spatial support, and a formal model-averaged forecast interval.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Using only open climate and land-cover data, we assessed and forecast ecological security in the Qilian Mountain region for 2005--2023 the analysis scripts, the processed result tables and a documented run order for which are publicly available, while of the five land-cover epochs only 2020 can be re-derived from the raw CLCD tile inside the package and the other four are supplied as an archived intermediate table. Under the eight-indicator primary specification the index peaks at 0.676 in 2010 and reaches 0.478 in 2023 (mild warning), and the nine-indicator series assigns the same grade in all 19 years while differing by at most 0.058; the grade distribution is 2 relatively-safe years, 16 mild-warning years and one medium-warning year. The conventional Mann--Kendall test finds no significant trend over the full record ($p=0.263$), whereas the 2010--2023 segment declines significantly under that specification ($p=0.006$, Sen's slope $-0.0086$\,yr$^{-1}$) but not under the eight-indicator one ($p=0.189$) and reverses sign under a six-indicator one; the post-2010 decline is therefore suggestive rather than established. Because the grades are relative to a study-specific normalisation range and to the indicator set, the warning output is delivered as a relative grade together with the continuous index and its uncertainty, not as a stand-alone trigger.

The 2024--2028 projection differs by model under the no-intervention scenario: GM(1,1) declines slowly from 0.469 to 0.448, the NGBM falls more steeply from 0.424 to 0.380 and ARIMA(0,1,1) is flat at 0.485, with an equal-weight mixture of 0.438 in 2028. The 2028 grade therefore depends on the model (mild warning for GM(1,1), ARIMA and the mixture; medium warning for the NGBM), and because every margin to the 0.400 boundary is of the same order as the rounding unit of the reported value, no 2028 grade is treated as a decision-relevant outcome. The NGBM value also depends on the exponent-selection window (0.420 with the full-series optimum of 0.05, 0.380 with the training-only optimum of 0.15), so the crossing is reported as exponent-conditional; the bootstrap 95\% interval for the NGBM is 0.172--0.615 and that for the mixture 0.166--0.945, the latter distorted at the upper end because the ARIMA interval reaches 1.113, above the support of the index. Under the nine-indicator baseline the same models project 0.493, 0.394 and 0.488, so the level of the projection is specification-dependent as well as model-dependent.
