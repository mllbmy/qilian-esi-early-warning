\begin{abstract}
Nature reserves are critical for biodiversity conservation, yet their management often lacks quantitative early-warning tools that translate monitoring data into actionable signals. We assess and forecast ecological security (ES) in the Qilian Mountain region (including the Qilian Mountain National Park), China, over 2005--2023, building on a Driving--Pressure--State--Impact--Response (DPSIR) indicator system and combining subjective--objective weighting, grey forecasting, and GeoDetector attribution, using only open climate (CRU TS) and land-cover (CLCD) data. The ecological security index (ESI) rose to a peak of 0.709 in 2010 (relatively safe), then declined and stabilized within the mild-warning band, reaching 0.486 in 2023; 16 of 19 years were in the mild-warning grade, and the 2023 grade was invariant to the subjective--objective weighting ratio. Forecasts for 2024--2028, obtained with the nonlinear grey Bernoulli model selected by a three-model comparison and holdout validation (2005--2018 fit, 2019--2023 held out), show a gradual decline from 0.446 to 0.394, with the grade lying between mild and medium warning. Spatially, the barren/degraded class ($q=0.80$) and the grassland class ($q=0.71$) have the highest explanatory power, followed by precipitation ($q=0.51$) and temperature ($q=0.17$) (all $p \le 0.001$), but all four are themselves components of the ESI; under a leave-one-target detection, in which the target ESI is recomputed after removing the factor under test, the first and last positions are unchanged (barren/degraded remains highest, temperature remains lowest), while grassland and precipitation swap the second and third positions---barren/degraded 0.711 $>$ precipitation 0.381 $>$ grassland 0.366 $>$ temperature 0.274 (all $p = 0.001$)---and the strictly exogenous terrain factors explain only 6--9\% of the variance (elevation $q=0.09$, slope $q=0.06$). We propose zonal management: strengthening grassland restoration in mild-warning zones and regulating cropland expansion, together with a pipeline transferable to other reserves whose analysis scripts, processed result tables, the ten figure files, and documented Stage~0--7 run order are openly available.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{Background}
Global biodiversity is declining at an unprecedented rate: vertebrate population sizes have fallen by an average of 68\% since 1970, terrestrial mammal biomass has decreased by at least 85\% since the late Pleistocene, and illegal wildlife trade is valued at up to USD 23 billion per year \citep{wwf2020living,ipcc2014climate}. Habitat loss, climate change, and poaching jointly threaten ecosystem stability. Protected areas (PAs) have become the cornerstone of \emph{in-situ} biodiversity conservation worldwide.

Yet the effectiveness of PAs is constrained by limited monitoring capacity. Conventional field surveys are labor-intensive and difficult to sustain over large or inaccessible areas; camera traps and remote sensing provide richer data but require systematic analytical frameworks to translate raw observations into management decisions \citep{kerry2022remote,bijl2022camera}. A recurring gap is the absence of \emph{early-warning} information: ecological degradation is often detected only after it has become severe, leaving little room for preventive intervention.

This monitoring-to-warning gap has been repeatedly identified by conservation initiatives. For example, international calls to reduce illegal wildlife trade have proposed cloud-based monitoring platforms that combine sensor networks, remote sensing, and artificial intelligence to deliver real-time alerts for habitat threats \citep{wwf2020living,kerry2022remote}; the early-warning decision module of such platforms typically rests on composite ecological-security indicators that compress multidimensional environmental information into an actionable signal. We formalize and empirically validate this warning step with real data.

An ecological security (ES) perspective offers such a systematic formalization. ES assessment compresses multidimensional environmental, social, and economic information into a composite index that can be tracked through time and space \citep{liu2023integrated,jinan_land_esp_2022}. We adopt this perspective and apply it to a national park where habitat integrity directly underpins wildlife conservation outcomes.

\subsection{Research status and knowledge gaps}
The pressure--state--response (PSR) and DPSIR frameworks are widely used to organize ES indicator systems \citep{changbai_dpsirm_2024,dpsir_dea_threegorges2023}. ES assessments at mountain and reserve scale include the Changbai Mountain region, Yunnan's underdeveloped areas, and the adjacent Lanzhou--Xining urban agglomeration \citep{lu2014changbai,ou2017yunnan,tong2020lanzhou}. Subjective weighting (AHP) \citep{saaty1980analytic}, objective weighting (entropy method), and their combinations are mainstream. Grey models (GM(1,1)) \citep{deng1982control} are favored for small-sample ES time series; Liu et al.\ integrated DPSIR, combined weights, GM(1,1), and GeoDetector into a reserve-scale early-warning framework \citep{liu2023integrated}. The GeoDetector method \citep{wang2016measure,wang2012environmental} quantifies the contribution of individual factors to spatial ES heterogeneity \citep{westliaohe_esp_2026,china_ecoenv_2024,qilian_ez_2024,zhang2019geodetector}.

Knowledge gaps remain: (1) reserve-scale studies usually apply the DPSIR--GM(1,1)--GeoDetector components in isolation or with a single weighting scheme; (2) the sensitivity of ES results to the weighting ratio $\alpha$ is rarely tested; (3) the suitability of GM(1,1) for non-monotonic ES series is seldom diagnosed; and (4) few studies translate early-warning results into zonal management recommendations.

\subsection{Objectives of this study}
This study aims to (1) quantify the 2005--2023 dynamics and warning state of ecological security in the Qilian Mountain region from open data; (2) test the robustness of warning grades to the subjective--objective weighting ratio ($\alpha = 0$--$1$); (3) diagnose the suitability of grey forecasting models for the non-monotonic ESI series and produce a 2024--2028 forecast; and (4) identify the spatial drivers of ecological security and derive zonal management recommendations.

\section{Study area and data}
\label{sec:study}

\subsection{Study area}
The Qilian Mountain region, centered on the Qilian Mountain National Park (QMNP), is located in the northeastern margin of the Qinghai--Tibet Plateau, spanning the border of Gansu and Qinghai provinces (Fig.~\ref{fig:area}). The park protects typical alpine ecosystems, including glaciers, snowfields, alpine meadows, and desert steppes, which provide critical water services for the Hexi Corridor and inland river basins \citep{gao2023water}. We analyze a rectangular region ($36.0$--$40.5^{\circ}$N, $93.5$--$103.5^{\circ}$E) centered on the park and its buffer counties; conclusions are interpreted at the regional scale. The study region has an annual mean temperature of 0.8--2.0\,$^{\circ}$C and annual precipitation of 167--284\,mm over 2005--2023. Analysis units include the whole region and 11 counties (Tianzhu, Sunan, Subei, Aksai, Minle, Shandan, Menyuan, Qilian, Gangcha, Delingha, Tianjun).

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{DPSIR framework and indicator system}
Following the DPSIR framework \citep{changbai_dpsirm_2024,dpsir_dea_threegorges2023}, nine indicators cover the criteria layers: Driving forces (precipitation, temperature), Pressure (barren/degraded proportion, cropland proportion), State (grassland, forest, water proportions), Impact (grassland change rate), and Response (ecological land proportion). The set relies exclusively on open, reproducible data (CRU TS 4.10 climate; CLCD 30\,m land cover) and captures both the climate forcing and the land-surface response that dominate ecological security in arid--semiarid alpine ecosystems. Temperature and precipitation are assigned positive directions because heat and moisture jointly limit vegetation growth in this cold, arid setting. The competing effect of warming through increased evaporative stress is examined in the spatial attribution (Sec.~\ref{sec:results}). Table~\ref{tab:indicators} lists all indicators with their directions. Fig.~\ref{fig:framework} outlines how the DPSIR causal chain connects to the ESI-based warning pipeline; all indicators are min--max normalized to $[0,1]$ before weighting (Sec.~\ref{sec:esi}).

\subsection{Combined weighting (AHP and entropy)}
Entropy weights are $e_j=-\frac{1}{\ln n}\sum_i p_{ij}\ln p_{ij}$ with $p_{ij}=x'_{ij}/\sum_i x'_{ij}$ and $w^{E}_j=(1-e_j)/\sum_j(1-e_j)$. AHP weights $w^{A}_j$ follow the principal-eigenvector method \citep{saaty1980analytic}. Because ecological security is most directly indicated by the State and Response layers, the criterion-level pairwise comparison matrix (five criteria on the Saaty 1--9 scale; Table~\ref{tab:ahp}) was derived from the DPSIR hierarchical priorities (State $>$ Response $>$ Impact $>$ Driving $=$ Pressure). This yields $\lambda_{\max}=5.015$, $CI=(\lambda_{\max}-n)/(n-1)=0.0038$ and $CR=CI/RI=0.0034<0.1$ ($RI=1.12$ at $n=5$), satisfying the consistency requirement. Within each criterion, indicators share the criterion weight equally. The combined weight is
\begin{equation}
w_j=\alpha\,w^{A}_j+(1-\alpha)\,w^{E}_j,\qquad \alpha\in[0,1],
\label{eq:weight}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha=0.4$ baseline and a full sensitivity analysis over $\alpha=0$--$1$ to test robustness to the subjective--objective balance.

\subsection{Ecological security index (ESI)}
\label{sec:esi}
Indicators are first min--max normalized into $[0,1]$:
\begin{equation}
x'_{ij}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{x_{ij}-\min_j}{\max_j-\min_j}, & \text{positive},\\[2mm]
\dfrac{\max_j-x_{ij}}{\max_j-\min_j}, & \text{negative},\end{cases}
\label{eq:norm}
\end{equation}
and the ESI is the weighted sum
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{ESI}_i=\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_j\,x'_{ij},\qquad \mathrm{ESI}\in[0,1].
\label{eq:esi}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Prediction models and warning classification}
The GM(1,1) model \citep{deng1982control} is built on the first-order accumulated series with least-squares estimates $(\hat a,\hat b)$:
\begin{equation}
\hat x^{(0)}(k+1)=\left(1-e^{\hat a}\right)\left(x^{(0)}(1)-\frac{\hat b}{\hat a}\right)e^{-\hat a k}.
\label{eq:gm}
\end{equation}
GM(1,1) assumes a monotonic exponential trend, which the observed ESI series violates (it rises to a peak in 2010 before declining); we therefore additionally apply the nonlinear grey Bernoulli model (NGBM), which generalizes GM(1,1) with a power exponent $\gamma$: $x^{(0)}(k)+a z^{(1)}(k)=b[z^{(1)}(k)]^{\gamma}$, and compare both with ARIMA. Model quality is evaluated by the posterior-variance ratio $C=S_2/S_1$ and the small-error probability $P$; grades: excellent ($C<0.35$, $P>0.95$), qualified ($C<0.50$, $P>0.80$), barely qualified ($C<0.65$, $P>0.70$). Forecast accuracy is additionally verified by holdout validation, in which each model is fitted on 2005--2018 and evaluated on the held-out 2019--2023 observations. The power exponent $\gamma$ of NGBM is determined by a grid search (0.05--1.95 in steps of 0.05, minimizing the residual sum of squares) and equals 0.20 in this case; the 95\% prediction intervals are constructed as the parametric $t$ interval $\sigma\cdot t(0.975,\,n-2)\cdot\sqrt{1+1/n}$, whose half-width does not increase with the forecast horizon, so the uncertainty of the 5-year forecast may be underestimated.

Forecasts and observed ESI values are interpreted against five warning levels: safe $[0.8,1]$, relatively safe $[0.6,0.8)$, mild warning $[0.4,0.6)$, medium warning $[0.2,0.4)$, and severe warning $[0,0.2)$. Thresholds are set at equal 0.2 intervals across the normalized range $[0,1]$, consistent with the five-grade warning convention used in DPSIR-based ecological-security early-warning frameworks for Chinese nature reserves \citep{liu2023integrated}; equal spacing keeps grades comparable across studies and avoids sample-dependent breakpoints, and the 2023 warning grade is shown below to be invariant to the weighting ratio.

\subsection{Spatial driving-factor identification (GeoDetector)}
Spatial attribution takes the 2020 point-scale ESI at 1{,}914 grid samples in the Qilian Mountain region as its target; the candidate factors are four---precipitation, temperature, grassland proportion, and barren/degraded proportion---each discretised into four equal-sample strata by sample quantiles (quartile stratification), and the $q$ statistic measures the share of the spatial variance of the ESI explained by that stratification. A parallel detection with four non-land-cover factors---precipitation, temperature, elevation, and slope---serves as a control. Of these four, only elevation and slope lie outside the composition of the ESI, so a strictly exogenous control rests on the terrain factors alone. Because precipitation, temperature, the grassland proportion, and the barren/degraded proportion are themselves ESI inputs, their baseline $q$ values contain a mechanical-correlation component shared with the target; a leave-one-target detection was therefore run in which the factor under test is removed from the six ESI components, the remaining five are re-normalised, re-weighted by entropy, re-weighted by AHP over the surviving criteria layers, and recombined at $\alpha=0.4$ to form the target ESI, against which that factor's $q$ is recomputed with the same stratification and 999 permutations. The ``barren/degraded'' factor is not the same column as the ESI input \texttt{barren\_neg}: the two disagree at 81 of the 1{,}914 points (75 cropland and 6 snow/ice points).
\begin{equation}
q=1-\frac{\sum_{h=1}^{L}N_h\sigma_h^2}{N\sigma^2},
\label{eq:q}
\end{equation}
where $q\in[0,1]$, with larger values indicating stronger explanatory power for the spatial differentiation of the ESI; $L$ is the number of strata, $N_h$ and $\sigma_h^2$ are the sample size and variance of stratum $h$, and $N$ and $\sigma^2$ are the total sample size and total variance of the ESI. Factor significance is tested by a permutation test (999 iterations) \citep{wang2016measure,wang2012environmental}; the interaction detector quantifies joint effects of factor pairs. At the grid scale, land-cover factors are represented by the dominant CLCD class within a $\pm$0.02° neighbourhood (binary categorical factors), whereas the county- and region-level indicators are computed as area fractions. Factors were sampled at 1{,}914 regular 0.15° grid points from the 2020 reference epoch of the CLCD series (consistent with the static county ESI) together with the 2005--2023 mean climate; temporal dynamics are quantified separately by the 19-year ESI series.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Fig.~\ref{fig:trends} shows the raw indicator dynamics behind the ESI. Precipitation fluctuates between 166.9 and 284.4\,mm\,yr$^{-1}$ and falls from 228.0 (2005) to 166.9\,mm (2023); mean temperature rises from 1.29 to 1.92\,$^{\circ}$C. The land-cover indicators, linearly interpolated between the five CLCD epochs, change slowly: ecological land proportion (grassland, forest, and water combined) rises from 37.9\% in 2005 to 39.6\% in 2010 and remains near 39.0\% in 2023, the barren/degraded proportion declines from 58.2\% to 56.3\% and then returns to 57.0\%, water (1.75--2.17\%, peaking in 2020 and declining thereafter) and forest (1.15--1.29\%) proportions increase overall, and cropland grows slightly (3.8--4.0\%). Grassland change rate is the most volatile signal ($-0.47$ to $+0.84$\%\,yr$^{-1}$). After normalization and combined weighting (Sec.~\ref{sec:esi}), these series yield the ESI trajectory analyzed below (Fig.~\ref{fig:esi}).

\subsection{ESI time series and warning grades (2005--2023)}
The regional ESI rose from 0.258 (medium warning) in 2005 to a peak of 0.709 (relatively safe) in 2010, then declined and fluctuated within 0.45--0.59 (mild warning), reaching 0.486 in 2023 (Fig.~\ref{fig:esi}). The warning-grade distribution over 19 years is 1 medium-warning year (2005), 2 relatively-safe years (2009, 2010), and 16 mild-warning years. A Mann--Kendall test finds no significant downward trend over the full 2005--2023 record ($Z=-1.120$, $p=0.263$; Sen's slope $-0.0032$\,yr$^{-1}$), so the region remains in a stable yet vulnerable mild-warning state. Threshold sensitivity was tested by perturbing the five-grade boundaries by $\pm$0.05: the 2023 grade remains mild warning under all three schemes, and in the historical series 6 years (thresholds shifted down) and 1 year (shifted up) change grade, whereas the 2028 forecast grade switches as a whole with the thresholds (all mild warning when shifted down, all medium warning when shifted up), so the absolute forecast grade alone should not be used as a decision basis.

\subsection{Forecast comparison: GM(1,1), NGBM, and ARIMA}
The 2024--2028 ESI forecast declines gradually from 0.446 to 0.394 under a no-intervention scenario, with parametric 95\% intervals of 0.358--0.533 (2024) and 0.306--0.482 (2028) that span the medium- and mild-warning grades, indicating non-negligible forecast uncertainty (Table~\ref{tab:forecast}; Fig.~\ref{fig:esi}). The forecast was produced with the nonlinear grey Bernoulli model (NGBM): among the three candidate models (Table~\ref{tab:forecast}), NGBM met the qualified accuracy grade (C $=$ 0.44, P $=$ 0.89), outperforming GM(1,1) (C $=$ 0.60, P $=$ 0.79, barely qualified) and ARIMA (C $=$ 1.09, unqualified), and holdout validation (fitted on 2005--2018, evaluated on the held-out 2019--2023 observations) confirmed its superiority (RMSE 0.034 vs.\ 0.065 for GM(1,1), a 47\% improvement), supporting the nonlinear grey-model choice for small-sample, non-stationary reserve-scale ES series \citep{althobaiti_ngbm2022}.

\subsection{Robustness to the weighting ratio $\alpha$}
Sensitivity analysis over $\alpha=0$--$1$ shows that the 2023 ESI increases from 0.425 (pure entropy, $\alpha=0$) to 0.578 (pure AHP, $\alpha=1$), while its warning grade remains ``mild warning'' throughout (Fig.~\ref{fig:alpha}). Across the 19-year series the number of mild-warning years falls from 16 at $\alpha=0.3$--$0.4$ to 5 at $\alpha\ge0.9$, and a severe-warning year appears once $\alpha\ge0.7$; the baseline $\alpha=0.4$ thus sits in the most stable regime. The early-warning conclusion for the current state is therefore robust to the subjective--objective weighting balance, although AHP-dominated weights amplify the spread of historical grades.

\subsection{County-level ESI, spatial driving factors, and obstacle factors}
The static county ESI (2020) uses the same combined weighting scheme ($\alpha = 0.4$) as the regional analysis, but the weights were recomputed from the 11-county cross-section: entropy weights were computed on the county cross-section, and AHP weights were set equal across the 6 available county-level indicators; the indicators are the 2020 land-cover proportions and the 2005--2023 climate means within a $\pm$0.1° window around each county centroid, min--max normalised across the 11 counties. It ranks Sunan (0.558) and Tianzhu (0.525) highest and Aksai (0.067) lowest (Table~\ref{tab:county}; Fig.~\ref{fig:spatial}). Eastern counties with higher grassland cover (Sunan, Tianzhu, Qilian) score higher than the arid western counties (Aksai, Delingha), although the pattern is modulated by local land-cover composition (e.g., the crop-dominated county Minle scores lower than some western counties). The gradient implies that conservation resources and early-warning priority should be allocated heterogeneously across the region.

GeoDetector on 1{,}914 grid samples shows that the barren/degraded class ($q=0.80$) and the grassland class ($q=0.71$) have the highest explanatory power, followed by precipitation ($q=0.51$), with temperature the weakest ($q=0.17$) (all $p \le 0.001$; permutation test, 999 iterations; Fig.~\ref{fig:qbar}). Under the leave-one-target detection (Sec.~3.5), which recomputes the target without the factor under test, the $q$ values of the four factors are 0.711 (barren/degraded), 0.381 (precipitation), 0.366 (grassland), and 0.274 (temperature), all $p = 0.001$: the first and last positions are unchanged (barren/degraded remains highest, temperature remains lowest), while grassland and precipitation swap the second and third positions; the barren/degraded class ranks above precipitation under both settings. The reduction is largest for grassland ($-48.2\%$), so a substantial part of its baseline $q$ comes from the mechanical correlation, and the advantage of precipitation over temperature narrows from 3.0-fold to 1.4-fold. The temperature $q$ rises from 0.170 to 0.274 ($+61.3\%$): this is a collinearity readout---$r(\text{temperature},\text{elevation})=-0.714$ and $r(\text{temperature},\text{barren/degraded})=+0.423$---rather than evidence that temperature is the stronger driver. The strictly exogenous terrain factors in the non-land-cover-factor control give elevation $q=0.0904$ and slope $q=0.0647$ (both $p=0.001$) and explain only 6--9\% of the point-scale variance; they are uncorrelated with each other ($r=-0.038$), so terrain alone does not account for the overall spatial pattern of the ESI. The interaction detector further shows that all factor pairs show \emph{enhancing} interactions: the joint effects of temperature$\times$barren ($q=0.849$), precipitation$\times$barren ($q=0.833$), and grassland$\times$barren ($q=0.818$) all exceed their individual contributions ($p \le 0.001$), indicating that thermal--arid and moisture--vegetation couplings compound spatial ES heterogeneity (Table~\ref{tab:interaction}; Fig.~\ref{fig:interaction}).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Ecological security changes and their drivers}
The 2005--2010 ESI recovery is consistent with large-scale ecological restoration (grazing-withdrawal and revegetation programs) and wetter years; the subsequent mild-warning plateau reflects persistent barren-dominated land cover ($\sim$56\%) and climate fluctuation. The dominant weighting of grassland dynamics corroborates the importance of alpine meadow ecosystems for regional ES, in line with findings that grassland degradation is the leading ecological-security constraint in alpine regions \citep{qilian_ez_2024,westliaohe_esp_2026}. The east--west county gradient and the GeoDetector results further indicate that precipitation has higher explanatory power than temperature under both settings (baseline 0.51 vs.\ 0.17; leave-one-target 0.38 vs.\ 0.27), a pattern consistent with arid-zone studies in which moisture availability, not thermal regime, limits ecosystem productivity \citep{china_ecoenv_2024}.

For wildlife and biodiversity conservation, the mild-warning state and the projected decline carry a concrete message: habitat integrity in the Qilian Mountain region is not improving, and monitoring programs that rely on static habitat assessment may miss slow degradation. The early-warning signal formalized here links raw environmental data to actionable warning grades, the decision-support input envisioned by recent wildlife-monitoring frameworks \citep{kerry2022remote,bijl2022camera}. The signal builds on the early-warning decision module of international wildlife-monitoring initiatives: composite ecological-security indicators convert remote-sensing and ground-monitoring data into actionable warning grades, closing the habitat monitoring--early-warning--response loop; we validate this framework empirically for the Qilian Mountain National Park.

\subsection{Comparison with previous studies}
Relative to the reserve-scale early-warning framework that combines DPSIR, combined weights, GM(1,1), and GeoDetector \citep{liu2023integrated}, our results confirm that this toolchain transfers to national-park regions, while extending it in the three respects summarized in Table~\ref{tab:compare}. First, the 2023 warning grade was invariant to the weighting ratio $\alpha$ over its full 0--1 range, whereas comparable DPSIR-based assessments report a single weighting scheme \citep{changbai_dpsirm_2024,dpsir_dea_threegorges2023}; the warning conclusion for the current state therefore does not depend on the analyst's weighting choice. Second, the observed ESI series is non-monotonic and violates the monotonic-trend assumption of GM(1,1); the nonlinear grey Bernoulli model captured this behavior at the qualified accuracy grade while ARIMA was unqualified, consistent with evidence that nonlinear grey variants suit short, non-stationary series \citep{althobaiti_ngbm2022}.

Third, linking the regional time series to grid- and county-level attribution reveals a scale dependence that single-scale studies cannot expose: the indicators dominating temporal variation (grassland change rate and cropland proportion; Table~\ref{tab:weights}) differ from those with the highest spatial explanatory power (the barren/degraded and grassland classes, both of which overlap the ESI inputs; Fig.~\ref{fig:qbar}). Early-warning design should therefore pair temporal monitoring of grassland and cropland dynamics with spatially targeted control of land degradation, rather than assuming one driver set serves both.

\subsection{Management implications}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Zonal priorities.} Mild-warning zones (dominant since 2011) should prioritize grassland restoration and grazing regulation, while the arid western counties (Aksai, Delingha) require desertification monitoring and water-resource management, not vegetation restoration \emph{per se}.
\item \emph{Cropland regulation.} Cropland proportion was the second-ranked obstacle in the plateau phase (0.206); expansion at the agropastoral ecotone should be controlled to curb land-use pressure.
\item \emph{Grade-based zonal response.} Management zoning is allocated by warning grade---whose bands span equal 0.2 intervals on the normalised range---and aligned with QMNP zoning rather than by micro-ranking of counties as a cross-county priority basis, operationalizing the early-warning concept for habitat and wildlife conservation; the thresholds serve only for the relative ordering of units within the study region and for cross-study comparison, and are neither ecological critical values nor a stand-alone trigger for automatic management response.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations and future work}
Limitations include the regional aggregation (the county index is static and derived from $\pm$0.1° centroid windows, so it represents conditions near the county centroid rather than county-wide averages, pending finer administrative boundaries), the interpolation of land cover between five epochs, the moderate accuracy of grey models on non-monotonic series (mitigated by the NGBM/ARIMA comparison), and the absence of independent cross-validation of the ESI against external ecological indicators (e.g., NDVI, field grass-cover observations). Four further structural issues arise. (1) The indicator system is internally correlated: the response-layer ecological land proportion is the sum of the grassland, forest, and water proportions, and the impact-layer grassland change rate is the annual change rate of the grassland proportion; the impact, state, and response layers together account for 69.29\% of the combined weight under the criterion-level AHP calibration used here (59.67\% under entropy weights only and 83.72\% under AHP weights only), so the criteria layers are not mutually independent. The barren/degraded proportion is likewise a linear identity in the remaining indicators (barren/degraded $=100-$ grassland $-$ forest $-$ water $-$ cropland, zero residual), so the collinear structure extends beyond the two identities noted above. (2) Spatial attribution contains an endogenous component: precipitation, temperature, the grassland proportion, and the barren/degraded proportion are themselves inputs to the ESI, so their $q$ values include mechanical correlation; the leave-one-target detection (Sec.~4.5) leaves the first and last positions unchanged while grassland and precipitation swap the second and third positions, with the largest reduction for grassland ($-48.2\%$). The point-scale land-cover factors are binary dominant-class indicators, so the barren/degraded class is by construction the complement of grassland, forest, and water; the 81 points dominated by cropland or snow/ice are therefore counted as barren/degraded. A leave-one-target detection was also recomputed from per-point fractional cover (\texttt{results\_fractional\_cover\_loo.csv}, scheme \texttt{fractional\_sigma1}, in the reproducibility package): grassland 0.584$\rightarrow$0.283 ($-51.6\%$), barren/degraded 0.838$\rightarrow$0.691 ($-17.6\%$), precipitation 0.600$\rightarrow$0.462, and temperature 0.178$\rightarrow$0.303 (all $p=0.001$). Under the one-hot calibration the leave-one-target $q$ values of the cover factors therefore serve as an upper-bound reference and cannot be read as evidence that a cover type is unimportant. The point-scale land-cover indicators are mutually exclusive classes of the same CLCD classification (represented by the dominant class), so they are not statistically independent by construction; when expressed as window-area fractions, the grassland and barren proportions are correlated at $r=-0.884$. The ESI variance is moreover dominated by sparse classes: forest (15 samples) and water (34 samples) together carry 0.609 of the combined weight (sample counts from \texttt{spatial\_samples.csv}; weights recomputed for the six point-scale ESI inputs at $\alpha=0.4$), i.e.\ 2.6\% of the samples carry 60.9\% of the weight. (3) The ESI is a relative index: its min--max normalisation depends on the study period and sample range, and the five-grade thresholds at equal 0.2 intervals facilitate cross-study comparison but carry no absolute ecological meaning. (4) The climate data are on a 0.5° grid, which introduces representativeness bias in the Qilian Mountains, where the terrain is complex and vertical differentiation is strong. In addition, the obstacle-degree analysis is likewise affected by indicator collinearity: the obstacle degree is proportional to $(1-x')\cdot w$, so the double counting embedded in the weights inflates it, and the obstacle degrees of the grassland change rate, water, and forest are no longer mutually independent. Moran's $I=0.643$ measures the spatial clustering of the ESI and does not by itself establish attribution, because the clustering may also arise from the spatial autocorrelation of the ESI components. Variables not included here (soil type, grazing pressure, human activity intensity) may have stronger explanatory power in the Qilian Mountains, and their omission biases the spatial attribution. The county ESI is a centroid-window statistic rather than a county-wide average. Recomputing it with four centroid windows ($\pm$0.05°, $\pm$0.10° (the baseline), $\pm$0.20°, and $\pm$0.30°) leaves the cross-county ordering unstable: against the baseline only 1 of the 11 counties keeps its rank, and across the four windows 7 of the 11 span three or more rank positions (Delingha, from 1st at $\pm$0.05° to 10th at $\pm$0.20°, spans nine; Sunan and Gangcha six each), while the Spearman rank correlation with the baseline is 0.682 ($\pm$0.05°), 0.509 ($\pm$0.20°), and 0.491 ($\pm$0.30°); the entropy weights are likewise window-dependent, with the water weight reaching 0.399 and 0.403 at $\pm$0.20° and $\pm$0.30°, under which Gangcha's water fraction rises from 0.046\% at $\pm$0.10° to 16.29\% at $\pm$0.20°, where the window first includes Qinghai Lake, and to 24.17\% at $\pm$0.30°. Aksai is the one county that keeps its rank: it is last under all four windows, has the highest barren fraction of the 11 counties under all four, and its ESI spans only 0.0201, so its low value is not a window artefact. Future work should (i) incorporate higher-temporal-resolution land cover and near-real-time warning updates, (ii) couple the ESI with wildlife population and habitat-quality indicators, (iii) extend the framework to a multi-reserve comparison to test transferability, and (iv) cross-validate the ESI against independent external indicators.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Using only open climate and land-cover data, we assessed and forecast ecological security in the Qilian Mountain region over 2005--2023 with an openly available pipeline (analysis scripts, processed result tables, figure files, and a documented Stage~0--7 run order). The regional ESI ranged 0.26--0.71 over 2005--2023; since 2011 the region has remained in a mild-warning state (2023 ESI $=$ 0.486), and the Mann--Kendall test found no significant downward trend over 2005--2023 ($p=0.263$), indicating a stable yet fragile state.

Forecasts for 2024--2028 indicate a gradual decline (NGBM: 0.446$\rightarrow$0.394) under a no-intervention scenario, warranting proactive intervention, with parametric 95\% intervals of 0.358--0.533 (2024) and 0.306--0.482 (2028); NGBM reached the qualified accuracy grade ($C=0.44$) with a holdout-validation RMSE of 0.034; the 95\% intervals span the mild- and medium-warning grades, so the forecast grade carries uncertainty, and this trend assumes no intervention and stable model parameters. The 2023 warning grade is robust to the subjective--objective weighting ratio (mild warning for all $\alpha=0$--$1$), but the historical grade distribution is sensitive to $\alpha$: the number of mild-warning years falls from 16 at $\alpha=0.3$--$0.4$ to 5 at $\alpha\ge0.9$, and one severe-warning year appears at $\alpha\ge0.7$; grassland dynamics and cropland pressure are the dominant signals. Spatially, the barren/degraded class ranks first under both settings (baseline $q=0.80$; leave-one-target $q=0.711$), followed by grassland (0.71 and 0.366), precipitation (0.51 and 0.381), and temperature (0.17 and 0.274), while the strictly exogenous terrain factors explain only 6--9\% of the point-scale variance. We recommend integrating ESI-based early warning into QMNP adaptive management, prioritizing grassland restoration in mild-warning zones and regulating cropland expansion; the framework is transferable to other nature reserves and provides early-warning--response decision support for wildlife habitat conservation and monitoring.
